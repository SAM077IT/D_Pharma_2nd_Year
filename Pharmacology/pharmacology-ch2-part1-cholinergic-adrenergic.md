## A. Steps Involved in Neurohumoral Transmission

**Definition:** Neurohumoral transmission is the process by which a nerve impulse is transmitted across a synapse/neuroeffector junction via chemical messengers (neurohumoral transmitters), rather than by direct electrical continuity.

**Steps (very high-yield — sequence question):**
1. **Synthesis** of the neurotransmitter (e.g., Acetylcholine from Choline + Acetyl-CoA via choline acetyltransferase; Noradrenaline from Tyrosine via a series of enzymatic steps)
2. **Storage** of the transmitter in synaptic vesicles at the nerve ending
3. **Release** of the transmitter into the synaptic cleft, triggered by the arrival of an action potential and calcium influx into the nerve terminal
4. **Binding** of the transmitter to specific receptors on the post-synaptic membrane, producing a biological response
5. **Termination of action** — by enzymatic degradation (e.g., Acetylcholinesterase breaks down ACh) and/or **reuptake** of the transmitter back into the presynaptic neuron (e.g., Noradrenaline reuptake)

**Two main neurohumoral transmitters of the peripheral autonomic nervous system:**
| Transmitter | Released by |
|---|---|
| Acetylcholine (ACh) | All preganglionic autonomic fibres, postganglionic parasympathetic fibres, somatic motor nerves to skeletal muscle |
| Noradrenaline (Norepinephrine) | Most postganglionic sympathetic fibres |

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: How is the action of Acetylcholine terminated at the synapse?</summary>
By the enzyme acetylcholinesterase, which rapidly hydrolyses ACh into choline and acetate — this is why anticholinesterase drugs (e.g., Neostigmine) prolong/enhance cholinergic effects.
</details>

## B. Cholinergic Drugs (Parasympathomimetics)

**Definition:** Drugs that mimic the action of acetylcholine at cholinergic receptors (muscarinic and/or nicotinic).

**Classification:**
| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Direct-acting (cholinergic receptor agonists) | Acetylcholine, Carbachol, Pilocarpine, Bethanechol |
| Indirect-acting (anticholinesterases — reversible) | Physostigmine, Neostigmine, Pyridostigmine, Edrophonium |
| Indirect-acting (anticholinesterases — irreversible) | Organophosphates (e.g., Echothiophate, and pesticides like Malathion/Parathion) |

**Mechanism of action:** Direct agonists bind and activate muscarinic/nicotinic receptors directly. Anticholinesterases inhibit acetylcholinesterase, preventing ACh breakdown and thereby prolonging/intensifying its action at the synapse.

**Pharmacological actions (mnemonic — "SLUDGE" for muscarinic overactivity):** **S**alivation, **L**acrimation, **U**rination, **D**efecation, **G**I upset, **E**mesis — plus bradycardia, bronchoconstriction, miosis (pupil constriction), increased GI/glandular secretions.

**Dose (representative):** Pilocarpine — 1–2% eye drops for glaucoma; Neostigmine — 15–30 mg orally every 3–4 hrs (myasthenia gravis) or 0.5–2.5 mg IM/IV (postoperative urinary retention/ileus, or reversal of non-depolarizing neuromuscular blockers).

**Indications:** Glaucoma (Pilocarpine — increases aqueous outflow), myasthenia gravis (Neostigmine, Pyridostigmine), postoperative urinary retention/paralytic ileus (Bethanechol, Neostigmine), reversal of non-depolarizing neuromuscular blockade (Neostigmine), diagnosis of myasthenia gravis (Edrophonium — short-acting).

**Contraindications:** Bronchial asthma/COPD (bronchoconstriction risk), peptic ulcer disease, GI or urinary tract obstruction (increases motility against obstruction), bradycardia/cardiac conduction defects, hyperthyroidism.

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: What is a "cholinergic crisis" and how does it present?</summary>
Excessive cholinergic stimulation from anticholinesterase overdose (e.g., in myasthenia gravis treatment or organophosphate poisoning) — presents with SLUDGE symptoms plus muscle weakness/paralysis (from persistent depolarization) and respiratory depression; treated with Atropine (blocks muscarinic effects) and Pralidoxime (regenerates acetylcholinesterase, useful in organophosphate poisoning).
</details>

## C. Anti-cholinergic Drugs (Parasympatholytics / Muscarinic Antagonists)

**Definition:** Drugs that block the action of acetylcholine at muscarinic receptors, thereby inhibiting parasympathetic effects.

**Classification/Examples:** Atropine (prototype), Hyoscine (Scopolamine), Ipratropium bromide, Tropicamide, Glycopyrrolate, Dicyclomine.

**Mechanism of action:** Competitively block muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, preventing ACh from producing its parasympathetic effects.

**Pharmacological actions:** Mydriasis (pupil dilation) and cycloplegia (loss of accommodation), tachycardia, bronchodilation and reduced secretions, reduced GI motility/secretions, reduced sweating, CNS effects (sedation/excitation depending on dose and agent).

**Dose (representative):** Atropine — 0.4–0.6 mg IM/IV (preanaesthetic medication or bradycardia); 1–2 mg IV in organophosphate poisoning (repeated as needed).

**Indications:** Preanaesthetic medication (reduces secretions), organophosphate poisoning (antidote), bradycardia/AV block, ophthalmic examination — mydriasis (Atropine/Tropicamide), irritable bowel syndrome/GI spasm (Dicyclomine), motion sickness (Hyoscine), COPD/asthma (Ipratropium — inhaled, minimal systemic absorption).

**Contraindications:** **Glaucoma** (can precipitate/worsen angle-closure glaucoma by pupil dilation), prostatic hypertrophy/urinary retention, tachyarrhythmias, paralytic ileus/GI obstruction.

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield PYQ: What is Atropine sulphate used as? / Define Mydriatics.</summary>
Atropine sulphate is used as a **mydriatic** drug (dilates the pupil) — also used as an antidote for organophosphate poisoning and to reduce secretions preoperatively. Mydriatics are drugs that dilate the pupil (by blocking the sphincter pupillae's muscarinic receptors, e.g., Atropine, Tropicamide) — opposite of miotics, which constrict the pupil.
</details>

## D. Adrenergic Drugs (Sympathomimetics)

**Definition:** Drugs that mimic the actions of the sympathetic neurotransmitters noradrenaline/adrenaline at adrenergic receptors.

**Classification (by receptor selectivity — high-yield):**
| Type | Examples | Main receptor |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha-1 agonists | Phenylephrine | α1 (vasoconstriction) |
| Alpha-2 agonists | Clonidine | α2 (central sympathetic inhibition) |
| Beta-1 agonists | Dobutamine | β1 (cardiac stimulation) |
| Beta-2 agonists | Salbutamol, Terbutaline | β2 (bronchodilation, uterine relaxation) |
| Non-selective/mixed | Adrenaline (α, β1, β2), Noradrenaline (α, β1), Dopamine (dose-dependent), Isoprenaline (β1, β2) | — |

**Mechanism of action:** Direct-acting agents bind adrenergic receptors directly (α1, α2, β1, β2); indirect-acting agents (e.g., Amphetamine, Tyramine) cause release of stored noradrenaline from nerve endings.

**Pharmacological actions:** Vasoconstriction (α1) or vasodilation in skeletal muscle (β2), increased heart rate/contractility (β1), bronchodilation (β2), pupil dilation (mydriasis, α1), reduced GI motility, glycogenolysis/hyperglycaemia.

**Dose (representative):** Adrenaline — 0.3–0.5 mg IM (anaphylaxis); Salbutamol — 100–200 mcg inhaled PRN (asthma).

**Indications:** Anaphylactic shock (Adrenaline — drug of choice), cardiogenic shock (Dopamine, Dobutamine), asthma/bronchospasm (Salbutamol, Terbutaline — β2 agonists), nasal decongestion (Phenylephrine), added to local anaesthetics to prolong action and reduce bleeding (Adrenaline — vasoconstriction), cardiac arrest (Adrenaline).

**Contraindications:** Hypertension, hyperthyroidism, cardiac arrhythmias, angle-closure glaucoma (mydriasis risk); caution with MAO inhibitors (exaggerated pressor response).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why is Adrenaline the drug of choice in anaphylaxis?</summary>
It acts on all three key receptor types simultaneously — α1 (reverses vasodilation/hypotension), β1 (increases cardiac output), and β2 (bronchodilation, reduces bronchospasm/laryngeal oedema) — addressing the multiple life-threatening components of anaphylaxis at once.
</details>

## E. Anti-adrenergic Drugs (Sympatholytics / Adrenergic Antagonists)

**Definition:** Drugs that block the effects of the sympathetic nervous system, either by blocking adrenergic receptors or by inhibiting noradrenaline release.

**Classification:**
| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Alpha-blockers (non-selective) | Phenoxybenzamine, Phentolamine |
| Alpha-1 selective blockers | Prazosin, Tamsulosin |
| Beta-blockers (non-selective) | Propranolol |
| Beta-1 selective blockers (cardioselective) | Atenolol, Metoprolol |
| Mixed alpha/beta blockers | Labetalol, Carvedilol |
| Indirect (sympathetic blocking agents, deplete/prevent NA release) | Reserpine, Guanethidine |

**Mechanism of action:** Competitively block alpha and/or beta adrenergic receptors, preventing noradrenaline/adrenaline from producing their sympathetic effects; indirect agents deplete neuronal noradrenaline stores or block its release.

**Pharmacological actions:** Alpha-blockers — vasodilation, ↓ BP, reduced urinary outflow resistance (useful in BPH — Tamsulosin); Beta-blockers — ↓ heart rate, ↓ contractility, ↓ BP, ↓ renin release, bronchoconstriction (non-selective agents).

**Dose (representative):** Propranolol — 40–80 mg orally twice daily (hypertension/angina); Prazosin — 1 mg orally 2–3 times daily (starting dose, hypertension/BPH).

**Indications:** Hypertension, angina (Beta-blockers), phaeochromocytoma (Phenoxybenzamine — preoperative alpha blockade), BPH (Alpha-1 blockers — Tamsulosin), arrhythmias, post-MI (Beta-blockers — mortality benefit), migraine prophylaxis, essential tremor, performance anxiety (Propranolol), glaucoma (Timolol — topical beta-blocker).

**Contraindications:** Beta-blockers — asthma/COPD (bronchoconstriction, especially non-selective agents), severe bradycardia/heart block, decompensated heart failure (acute); Alpha-blockers — caution with first-dose hypotension (postural hypotension, especially Prazosin).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why are non-selective beta-blockers avoided in asthma/COPD?</summary>
They block β2 receptors in the bronchi (in addition to cardiac β1), causing bronchoconstriction and worsening airway obstruction — cardioselective (β1-selective) agents like Atenolol are relatively safer, but still used cautiously.
</details>
