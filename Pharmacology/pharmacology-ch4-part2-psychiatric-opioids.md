## A. Anti-Depressant Drugs

**Definition:** Drugs used to treat depression by correcting the deficiency of monoamine neurotransmitters (serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine) at CNS synapses.

**Classification (very high-yield — "Define and classify antidepressants" is a recurring exam question):**
| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) | Imipramine, Amitriptyline, Clomipramine |
| Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs) | Fluoxetine, Sertraline, Escitalopram |
| Serotonin-Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibitors (SNRIs) | Venlafaxine, Duloxetine |
| Monoamine Oxidase Inhibitors (MAOIs) | Phenelzine, Tranylcypromine |
| Atypical antidepressants | Mirtazapine, Bupropion |

**Mechanism of action:**
- **TCAs:** Block reuptake of both serotonin and norepinephrine at the presynaptic neuron, increasing their synaptic availability; also block muscarinic, histaminic, and alpha-adrenergic receptors (source of side effects)
- **SSRIs:** Selectively block serotonin reuptake — fewer side effects than TCAs, now generally first-line
- **MAOIs:** Inhibit monoamine oxidase, the enzyme that breaks down serotonin/norepinephrine/dopamine, increasing their levels

**Dose (representative):** Fluoxetine — 20 mg orally once daily; Amitriptyline — 25–150 mg orally at bedtime (also used at lower doses for neuropathic pain).

**Indications:** Major depressive disorder, anxiety disorders (SSRIs/SNRIs), neuropathic pain (TCAs — low dose), enuresis in children (Imipramine, historical use), OCD (Clomipramine, SSRIs).

**Contraindications/cautions:** TCAs — cardiotoxic in overdose (arrhythmias), anticholinergic side effects (dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, urinary retention), avoid in cardiac conduction defects, narrow-angle glaucoma; MAOIs — **dietary tyramine restriction required** (aged cheese, cured meats, wine — risk of hypertensive crisis), many drug interactions (avoid with SSRIs — serotonin syndrome risk); all antidepressants — delayed onset of therapeutic effect (2–4 weeks), monitor for suicidal ideation especially early in treatment (esp. adolescents/young adults).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield PYQ: Define and classify antidepressants. Describe the action of tricyclic antidepressants.</summary>
Antidepressants are drugs used to treat depression by increasing the availability of monoamine neurotransmitters (serotonin, norepinephrine) in the CNS. **Classification:** TCAs, SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, Atypical agents (see table above). **TCAs (e.g., Imipramine, Amitriptyline):** Block reuptake of both serotonin and norepinephrine at the presynaptic nerve terminal, increasing synaptic concentration and enhancing neurotransmission; they also block muscarinic, histaminic (H1), and alpha-adrenergic receptors, which explains side effects like dry mouth, sedation, and postural hypotension. Cardiotoxic in overdose — a key safety concern.
</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: What is serotonin syndrome and when can it occur?</summary>
A potentially life-threatening state of excess serotonergic activity — presenting with agitation, hyperthermia, tremor, hyperreflexia, and autonomic instability. Risk increases when serotonergic drugs are combined (e.g., an SSRI with an MAOI, or two serotonergic agents together) — MAOIs must be stopped for a wash-out period before starting an SSRI.
</details>

## B. Anti-Psychotic Drugs

**Definition:** Drugs used to manage psychotic disorders (e.g., schizophrenia) by reducing hallucinations, delusions, and disordered thinking — primarily by blocking dopamine (D2) receptors.

**Classification (high-yield — Typical vs Atypical):**
| Type | Examples | Feature |
|---|---|---|
| Typical (1st-generation) | Chlorpromazine, Haloperidol, Trifluoperazine | Strong D2 blockade — effective for positive symptoms, higher risk of extrapyramidal side effects (EPS) |
| Atypical (2nd-generation) | Risperidone, Olanzapine, Clozapine, Quetiapine | Block D2 and serotonin (5-HT2A) receptors — lower EPS risk, help negative symptoms too; more metabolic side effects |

**Mechanism of action:** Primarily block dopamine D2 receptors in the mesolimbic pathway (reducing positive symptoms — hallucinations, delusions); atypical agents additionally block 5-HT2A serotonin receptors, which modulates dopamine blockade in the nigrostriatal pathway (reducing EPS risk).

**Dose (representative):** Haloperidol — 0.5–5 mg orally 2–3 times daily (adjusted to response); Olanzapine — 5–20 mg orally once daily.

**Indications:** Schizophrenia, acute mania/bipolar disorder, severe agitation, Chlorpromazine — also used as an antiemetic (in low doses) and for intractable hiccups.

**Contraindications/cautions:** **Extrapyramidal side effects (EPS)** — acute dystonia, akathisia, drug-induced Parkinsonism, tardive dyskinesia (long-term, may be irreversible) — more common with typical agents; **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome** — rare but life-threatening (hyperthermia, muscle rigidity, autonomic instability); Clozapine — risk of **agranulocytosis**, requires mandatory regular blood count monitoring; metabolic syndrome (weight gain, dyslipidaemia, diabetes risk) — more common with atypical agents (esp. Olanzapine, Clozapine).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: What are extrapyramidal side effects (EPS)? Name the drugs causing them.</summary>
EPS are movement disorders arising from excessive dopamine D2 receptor blockade in the nigrostriatal pathway — include acute dystonia (sustained muscle spasm), akathisia (motor restlessness), drug-induced Parkinsonism (tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia), and tardive dyskinesia (involuntary, often irreversible orofacial movements with long-term use). Most associated with typical/1st-generation antipsychotics — Haloperidol, Chlorpromazine, Trifluoperazine.
</details>

## C. Nootropic Agents

**Definition:** Drugs believed to enhance cognitive function — memory, learning, and mental performance — sometimes called "cognitive enhancers" or "smart drugs."

**Classification/Examples:** Piracetam (prototype), Modafinil, Donepezil (cholinesterase inhibitor, used specifically in Alzheimer's dementia), Citicoline, Ginkgo biloba (herbal, adjunct).

**Mechanism of action:** Not fully unified across the class; Piracetam is thought to enhance neuronal membrane fluidity and cerebral blood flow, and modulate neurotransmission (exact mechanism unclear); Donepezil specifically inhibits acetylcholinesterase, increasing ACh availability (relevant in Alzheimer's, where cholinergic neurons are lost).

**Dose (representative):** Piracetam — 1.2–4.8 g/day orally in divided doses.

**Indications:** Cognitive decline/dementia (Donepezil — specifically for Alzheimer's disease), post-stroke rehabilitation (Piracetam, adjunctive), myoclonus (Piracetam has a specific licensed use here), attention/wakefulness disorders (Modafinil — narcolepsy).

**Contraindications/cautions:** Generally well-tolerated; Piracetam — caution in severe renal impairment (renally excreted); overall evidence for cognitive benefit in healthy individuals is limited — main solid evidence base is in specific clinical conditions (e.g., Donepezil in Alzheimer's).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: What distinguishes Piracetam's use from Donepezil's use, despite both being called cognitive-enhancing agents?</summary>
Piracetam has a broad, not-fully-defined mechanism and is used adjunctively in conditions like post-stroke recovery and myoclonus. Donepezil has a specific, well-defined mechanism (acetylcholinesterase inhibition) and an evidence-based indication specifically in Alzheimer's disease dementia, where cholinergic neuron loss is central to the pathology.
</details>

## D. Centrally Acting Muscle Relaxants

**Definition:** Drugs that reduce skeletal muscle tone/spasm by acting on the CNS (spinal cord or brain), as opposed to peripherally-acting neuromuscular blockers (see Chapter 2).

**Classification/Examples:** **Diazepam** (Benzodiazepine — enhances GABA-A mediated inhibition, widely used), Baclofen (GABA-B receptor agonist — used especially in spasticity from spinal cord injury/MS), Tizanidine (α2-adrenergic agonist), Cyclobenzaprine, Methocarbamol.

**Mechanism of action:** Act at the level of the spinal cord and/or brainstem to reduce excessive muscle tone/reflex activity — Diazepam via GABA-A potentiation; Baclofen via GABA-B receptor agonism (inhibits excitatory neurotransmitter release in the spinal cord); Tizanidine via central α2-adrenergic agonism (reduces excitatory input to motor neurons).

**Dose (representative):** Diazepam — 2–10 mg orally 2–4 times daily (muscle spasm indication); Baclofen — 5 mg orally 3 times daily, titrated upward.

**Indications:** Muscle spasm/spasticity (from causes like cerebral palsy, multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injury), acute low back pain with muscle spasm, tetanus-associated spasm (Diazepam).

**Contraindications/cautions:** Sedation is a common side effect across this class (caution with driving/machinery); Diazepam — dependence risk with prolonged use; Baclofen — avoid abrupt withdrawal (risk of seizures/hallucinations); caution in hepatic/renal impairment.

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield PYQ: Which drug is used as a centrally acting muscle relaxant?</summary>
Diazepam is a classic example (Benzodiazepine, acts centrally via GABA-A enhancement) — distinguish this from peripherally-acting neuromuscular blockers like D-Tubocurarine, which act directly at the neuromuscular junction rather than centrally.
</details>

## E. Opioid Analgesics

**Definition:** Drugs that relieve pain by acting on opioid receptors in the CNS, mimicking the action of endogenous opioid peptides (endorphins/enkephalins).

**Classification (high-yield):**
| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Natural opioids | **Morphine**, Codeine |
| Semi-synthetic | Diacetylmorphine (Heroin), Oxycodone |
| Synthetic | Pethidine (Meperidine), Fentanyl, Methadone, Tramadol |
| Opioid antagonists | Naloxone, Naltrexone |
| Mixed agonist-antagonists | Pentazocine, Buprenorphine |

**Mechanism of action:** Bind to opioid receptors (mainly **mu (μ) receptors**, also kappa and delta) in the CNS and periphery, mimicking endogenous opioids — inhibit neurotransmitter release and neuronal excitability, reducing pain perception and the emotional response to pain.

**Pharmacological actions:** Strong analgesia, sedation, euphoria, respiratory depression (dose-dependent, the usual cause of death in overdose), miosis (pinpoint pupils — classic diagnostic sign), constipation (decreased GI motility), cough suppression (Codeine — antitussive use), nausea/vomiting.

**Dose (representative):** Morphine — 5–10 mg IM/SC every 4 hours (acute severe pain) or oral formulations for chronic/cancer pain, titrated to effect.

**Indications:** Severe acute pain (post-surgical, trauma, MI), chronic cancer pain (WHO analgesic ladder — step 3), pulmonary oedema (Morphine — reduces preload/anxiety), cough suppression (Codeine, low dose), diarrhoea (Loperamide — peripherally-acting opioid agonist, minimal CNS penetration).

**Contraindications/cautions:** Respiratory depression/failure, head injury (masks neurological signs, raises intracranial pressure via CO2 retention), avoid in acute asthma exacerbation, caution in hepatic/renal impairment (accumulation), risk of tolerance/physical dependence with prolonged use, constipation is near-universal (co-prescribe a laxative for chronic use).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield PYQ: Morphine is a ____________ analgesic.</summary>
Narcotic (opioid) analgesic — acting primarily via μ (mu) opioid receptors in the CNS.
</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: What is the antidote for opioid overdose, and how does it work?</summary>
Naloxone — a competitive opioid receptor antagonist that rapidly reverses opioid effects, including life-threatening respiratory depression, by displacing the opioid from its receptor. Its duration of action is often shorter than that of the opioid causing overdose, so repeated dosing/monitoring may be needed.
</details>
