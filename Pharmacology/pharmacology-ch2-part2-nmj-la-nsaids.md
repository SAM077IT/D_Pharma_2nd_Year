## A. Neuromuscular Blocking Agents

**Definition:** Drugs that block transmission at the neuromuscular junction (skeletal muscle), producing muscle paralysis — used mainly as an adjunct to general anaesthesia for surgical muscle relaxation.

**Classification:**
| Type | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Non-depolarizing (competitive) | Competitively block ACh at nicotinic (N2) receptors on the motor end-plate — **reversible** with anticholinesterases | D-Tubocurarine, Pancuronium, Vecuronium, Atracurium, Rocuronium |
| Depolarizing | Bind and persistently depolarize the motor end-plate, causing initial fasciculations then flaccid paralysis (receptor becomes unresponsive) — **not reversed** by anticholinesterases (may worsen it) | Succinylcholine (Suxamethonium) |

**Pharmacological actions:** Flaccid paralysis of skeletal muscle in a predictable sequence — small, fast-moving muscles (eyes, face, fingers) paralyzed first, then limbs/trunk, finally the diaphragm (respiratory muscles) — respiration must be supported (ventilator) once diaphragm is affected.

**Dose:** Highly individualized, titrated to effect under anaesthesia monitoring (e.g., Vecuronium 0.08–0.1 mg/kg IV) — not typically expected as a fixed number in D.Pharm theory exams; focus is on classification/mechanism/reversal.

**Indications:** Muscle relaxation for surgery (as an adjunct to general anaesthesia), facilitating endotracheal intubation, mechanical ventilation in ICU (selected cases), management of tetanus-related muscle spasm.

**Contraindications/cautions:** Myasthenia gravis (exaggerated/prolonged response to non-depolarizing agents), Succinylcholine — contraindicated in hyperkalaemia, malignant hyperthermia susceptibility (rare but life-threatening reaction), major burns/muscle trauma (risk of severe hyperkalaemia); must always be used with facilities for respiratory support (ventilation) available.

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield PYQ-style: How is the action of non-depolarizing neuromuscular blockers reversed?</summary>
By anticholinesterase drugs (e.g., Neostigmine), which increase ACh concentration at the neuromuscular junction, allowing ACh to out-compete the blocker at nicotinic receptors — Neostigmine is typically co-administered with an anticholinergic (Atropine/Glycopyrrolate) to prevent its muscarinic side effects.
</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Write a short note on D-Tubocurarine.</summary>
Prototype non-depolarizing neuromuscular blocker; competitively blocks nicotinic (N2) receptors at the motor end-plate, preventing ACh-induced depolarization → flaccid paralysis. Used historically as a muscle relaxant during surgery; action reversible with anticholinesterases (e.g., Neostigmine). Can cause histamine release → hypotension, bronchospasm.
</details>

## B. Drugs Used in Myasthenia Gravis

**Background:** Myasthenia gravis is an autoimmune disease where antibodies attack nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction, reducing the number of functional receptors and causing fluctuating skeletal muscle weakness/fatigue.

**Classification of drugs used:**
| Category | Examples | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Anticholinesterases (symptomatic treatment — mainstay) | **Pyridostigmine**, Neostigmine | Increase ACh availability at the remaining receptors, improving muscle strength |
| Short-acting anticholinesterase (diagnostic) | Edrophonium | Rapid-onset, short-duration — used for diagnostic "Edrophonium/Tensilon test" |
| Immunosuppressants (disease-modifying) | Corticosteroids (Prednisolone), Azathioprine | Reduce the autoimmune antibody attack on receptors |
| Other therapies | Plasmapheresis, IV Immunoglobulin (IVIG), Thymectomy | Used in severe/refractory disease or myasthenic crisis |

**Mechanism of action (Pyridostigmine/Neostigmine):** Inhibit acetylcholinesterase, increasing the amount and duration of ACh available at the neuromuscular junction, partially compensating for the reduced number of functional receptors.

**Dose (representative):** Pyridostigmine — 60 mg orally every 4–6 hours (adjusted to symptom control).

**Indications:** Symptomatic control of myasthenia gravis (Pyridostigmine — first-line), diagnosis (Edrophonium test), severe exacerbations/myasthenic crisis (immunosuppressants, plasmapheresis, IVIG).

**Contraindications/cautions:** Overdose of anticholinesterases can precipitate a **cholinergic crisis** (which paradoxically also causes muscle weakness, confusingly similar to a myasthenic crisis — differentiated by Edrophonium test response); caution with drugs that impair neuromuscular transmission (e.g., aminoglycosides, some anaesthetics).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: How does the Edrophonium (Tensilon) test help differentiate myasthenic crisis from cholinergic crisis?</summary>
Edrophonium (a very short-acting anticholinesterase) transiently increases ACh availability. In a **myasthenic crisis** (too little cholinergic activity), muscle strength briefly *improves*. In a **cholinergic crisis** (anticholinesterase overdose, too much cholinergic activity), muscle weakness *worsens* further — this response distinguishes the two, which require opposite management.
</details>

## C. Local Anaesthetic Agents

**Definition:** Drugs that reversibly block nerve conduction (sensory, and at higher concentration motor) in a localized area of the body, without causing loss of consciousness.

**Classification (by chemical structure — high-yield):**
| Group | Examples | Metabolism |
|---|---|---|
| Esters | Procaine, Cocaine, Benzocaine, Tetracaine | Hydrolyzed by plasma pseudocholinesterase; higher allergy potential |
| Amides | **Lidocaine**, Bupivacaine, Prilocaine, Ropivacaine | Metabolized in the liver; lower allergy potential — more commonly used today |

**Mechanism of action:** Block voltage-gated sodium channels in the nerve membrane, preventing sodium influx needed for action potential generation/propagation — this blocks nerve impulse conduction in the affected area (reversible).

**Pharmacological effects:** Loss of pain sensation first, followed by temperature, touch, and finally motor function as concentration/duration increases (differential blockade — small unmyelinated pain fibres are blocked first).

**Dose (representative):** Lidocaine — 0.5–2% solution for infiltration anaesthesia (dose depends on site/procedure, max ~4.5 mg/kg without adrenaline).

**Indications:** Infiltration anaesthesia (minor surgery, dental procedures), nerve block anaesthesia, spinal/epidural anaesthesia, topical anaesthesia (mucous membranes, skin), Lidocaine also used IV as a **Class Ib anti-arrhythmic** (post-MI ventricular arrhythmias).

**Contraindications/cautions:** Known hypersensitivity (more common with ester type), avoid injection into inflamed/infected tissue (reduced efficacy — acidic environment), caution with cardiac conduction defects, avoid high doses in patients with hepatic impairment (amides); **Adrenaline is often co-administered** to cause local vasoconstriction — prolongs anaesthetic action and reduces bleeding, but must be avoided in end-arterial areas (fingers, toes, nose, ears, penis — risk of ischaemia).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield PYQ: Which anti-anginal drug also acts as a local anaesthetic?</summary>
Lidocaine — primarily a local anaesthetic (amide type, sodium channel blocker) but also used as a Class Ib anti-arrhythmic drug (note: it is an anti-arrhythmic rather than classically "anti-anginal," but this exact phrasing has appeared in past papers — the key fact to remember is that Lidocaine's sodium-channel-blocking action gives it dual local anaesthetic and anti-arrhythmic use).
</details>

## D. Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs)

**Definition:** A group of drugs with analgesic, antipyretic, and anti-inflammatory properties, acting mainly by inhibiting the cyclooxygenase (COX) enzyme.

**Classification:**
| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Salicylates | Aspirin |
| Propionic acid derivatives | Ibuprofen, Naproxen |
| Acetic acid derivatives | Diclofenac, Indomethacin |
| Fenamates | Mefenamic acid |
| Oxicams | Piroxicam |
| Selective COX-2 inhibitors | Celecoxib, Etoricoxib |
| Para-aminophenol derivative (analgesic/antipyretic, weak anti-inflammatory) | Paracetamol (often grouped separately) |

**Mechanism of action:** Inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX-1 and/or COX-2) enzymes, blocking the conversion of arachidonic acid to prostaglandins — reducing prostaglandin-mediated pain, fever, and inflammation. **COX-1** is largely constitutive (protective — gastric mucosa, platelet function); **COX-2** is mainly induced during inflammation — this distinction explains the adverse effect profile.

**Dose (representative):** Ibuprofen — 400 mg orally every 6–8 hours; Aspirin — 300–600 mg orally every 4–6 hours (analgesic/antipyretic dose) or 75–150 mg once daily (antiplatelet dose).

**Indications:** Mild-moderate pain (headache, musculoskeletal pain, dysmenorrhea), fever, inflammatory conditions (rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, gout), low-dose Aspirin — antiplatelet use in cardiovascular disease prevention.

**Contraindications:** Peptic ulcer disease/GI bleeding history (COX-1 inhibition reduces protective gastric mucus/bicarbonate), renal impairment (prostaglandins maintain renal blood flow — NSAIDs can precipitate acute kidney injury), asthma (aspirin-sensitive asthma — bronchospasm risk), third trimester of pregnancy (risk of premature closure of ductus arteriosus), bleeding disorders (antiplatelet effect, especially Aspirin — irreversible COX inhibition), children with viral infections (Aspirin — Reye's syndrome risk).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why do selective COX-2 inhibitors (e.g., Celecoxib) cause fewer GI side effects than traditional NSAIDs?</summary>
They spare COX-1, which is responsible for producing protective prostaglandins in the gastric mucosa (maintaining mucus/bicarbonate secretion and mucosal blood flow) — by selectively inhibiting only COX-2 (the inflammation-associated isoform), gastric protection is largely preserved. (Note: COX-2 selective agents carry an increased cardiovascular risk instead.)
</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why is Aspirin specifically avoided in children with fever due to viral illness?</summary>
Risk of Reye's syndrome — a rare but serious condition causing acute encephalopathy and liver damage in children given Aspirin during a viral infection (e.g., chickenpox, influenza).
</details>
