## A. Introduction & Basic Principles of Chemotherapy

**Chemotherapy — definition:** Use of chemical agents to destroy infecting organisms (bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoa, helminths) or neoplastic cells **without significantly harming the host** — the concept of **selective toxicity**.

**Selective toxicity:** The ability of a drug to injure/kill a pathogen or cancer cell while causing minimal/no harm to host cells — achieved by exploiting biochemical differences between pathogen and host (e.g., bacterial cell wall synthesis — absent in human cells — is why beta-lactams are safe and effective).

**Key terms (very high-yield, asked as 1-mark/definition questions):**
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Bacteriostatic | Inhibits bacterial growth/multiplication (host immunity clears the rest) — e.g., Tetracyclines, Sulphonamides, Macrolides |
| Bactericidal | Directly kills bacteria — e.g., Penicillins, Cephalosporins, Aminoglycosides, Fluoroquinolones |
| Spectrum of activity | Range of organisms a drug is effective against — narrow-spectrum (few species) vs broad-spectrum (wide range, Gram +ve & –ve) |
| Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) | Lowest concentration of drug that inhibits visible bacterial growth |
| Chemoprophylaxis | Use of a chemotherapeutic agent to prevent infection (e.g., antimalarial prophylaxis for travellers) |
| Drug resistance | Loss of susceptibility of an organism to a drug that was previously effective |
| Superinfection | New infection appearing during treatment of a primary infection, due to disturbance of normal microbial flora (common with broad-spectrum antibiotics) |
| Chemotherapeutic index | Ratio of the toxic dose to the therapeutic dose — a measure of drug safety |

**General principles/basis for selecting a chemotherapeutic agent:**
1. Identification of the causative organism (culture & sensitivity where possible)
2. Selecting the drug with the narrowest effective spectrum (reduces resistance/superinfection risk)
3. Host factors — age, renal/hepatic function, pregnancy, allergy history, immune status
4. Site of infection — drug must reach adequate concentration at the infection site (e.g., CNS penetration for meningitis)
5. Adequate dose, route, and duration — **under-dosing/incomplete courses promote resistance**
6. Cost and availability

**Mechanisms of antimicrobial action (classify, high-yield):**
| Mechanism | Example drug classes |
|---|---|
| Inhibition of cell wall synthesis | Penicillins, Cephalosporins |
| Inhibition of protein synthesis | Aminoglycosides, Tetracyclines, Macrolides |
| Inhibition of nucleic acid synthesis | Fluoroquinolones, Rifampicin |
| Inhibition of folate synthesis (antimetabolites) | Sulphonamides |
| Disruption of cell membrane | Polymyxins, Antifungal polyenes |

**Mechanisms of drug resistance (high-yield):**
- Production of drug-inactivating enzymes (e.g., beta-lactamase against penicillins)
- Altered target site (e.g., altered penicillin-binding proteins in MRSA)
- Decreased drug permeability/uptake into the microbial cell
- Increased efflux of the drug out of the cell
- Development of an alternative metabolic pathway

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Differentiate bacteriostatic and bactericidal with one example each.</summary>
Bacteriostatic drugs inhibit bacterial growth/multiplication without killing them (host defenses clear the infection) — e.g., Tetracycline. Bactericidal drugs directly kill the bacteria — e.g., Penicillin.
</details>

## B. Penicillins

**Classification (with examples):**
| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Natural penicillins | Benzylpenicillin (Penicillin G), Phenoxymethylpenicillin (Penicillin V) |
| Penicillinase-resistant | Methicillin, Cloxacillin, Dicloxacillin |
| Extended-spectrum (aminopenicillins) | Ampicillin, Amoxicillin |
| Antipseudomonal (extended-spectrum) | Carbenicillin, Piperacillin |

**Mechanism of action:** Inhibit bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding to penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), blocking the transpeptidation (cross-linking) step of peptidoglycan synthesis → cell lysis (bactericidal).

**Dose (representative, adult):** Benzylpenicillin — 0.6–1.2 g (1–2 million units) IM/IV every 6 hours; Amoxicillin — 250–500 mg orally every 8 hours.

**Indications:** Streptococcal infections (pharyngitis, rheumatic fever prophylaxis), pneumococcal pneumonia, syphilis (Benzathine penicillin G), meningococcal meningitis, gas gangrene, respiratory/UTI infections (Amoxicillin — broader spectrum).

**Contraindications/cautions:** Known penicillin hypersensitivity/allergy (risk of anaphylaxis); caution in renal impairment (dose adjustment).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why are penicillins combined with beta-lactamase inhibitors (e.g., Amoxicillin + Clavulanic acid)?</summary>
Many resistant bacteria produce beta-lactamase enzymes that destroy the penicillin ring. Clavulanic acid inhibits this enzyme, protecting the penicillin and restoring/extending its activity against resistant organisms.
</details>

## C. Cephalosporins

**Classification (by generation — very high-yield, frequently asked as MCQ):**
| Generation | Examples | Notable feature |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | Cefazolin, Cephalexin | Good Gram +ve cover, surgical prophylaxis |
| 2nd | Cefuroxime, **Cefaclor** | Extended Gram –ve cover |
| 3rd | Ceftriaxone, Cefotaxime, Cefixime | Wide Gram –ve cover, good CNS penetration (Ceftriaxone — meningitis) |
| 4th | Cefepime | Extended spectrum, active against Pseudomonas |
| 5th | Ceftaroline | MRSA cover |

**Mechanism of action:** Similar to penicillins — inhibit cell wall synthesis via PBP binding (bactericidal); beta-lactam ring structurally related to penicillins.

**Dose (representative):** Ceftriaxone — 1–2 g IV/IM once daily; Cephalexin — 250–500 mg orally every 6 hours.

**Indications:** Respiratory tract infections, UTI, skin/soft tissue infections, surgical prophylaxis (1st gen), meningitis/typhoid/gonorrhoea (3rd gen — Ceftriaxone).

**Contraindications/cautions:** Hypersensitivity to cephalosporins; partial cross-reactivity risk in patients with severe penicillin allergy (caution); dose adjustment in renal impairment.

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield PYQ: Cefaclor belongs to which generation of cephalosporins?</summary>
Second generation.
</details>

## D. Aminoglycosides

**Classification/Examples:** Streptomycin, Gentamicin, Amikacin, Tobramycin, Neomycin, Kanamycin.

**Mechanism of action:** Irreversibly bind to the bacterial 30S ribosomal subunit → inhibit protein synthesis (cause misreading of mRNA) — **bactericidal**; effective mainly against aerobic Gram-negative bacilli.

**Dose (representative):** Gentamicin — 3–5 mg/kg/day IV/IM in divided doses; Streptomycin — 15 mg/kg/day IM (used in TB regimens).

**Indications:** Severe Gram-negative infections (septicaemia, complicated UTI), often combined with a beta-lactam for synergy (e.g., in endocarditis); Streptomycin — as part of anti-TB therapy.

**Contraindications/cautions:** **Ototoxicity** (auditory/vestibular damage — often irreversible) and **nephrotoxicity** — avoid in renal impairment/pregnancy where possible; avoid combining with other oto/nephrotoxic drugs; caution in myasthenia gravis (neuromuscular blockade risk).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Two major dose-limiting toxicities of aminoglycosides?</summary>
Ototoxicity (irreversible hearing/balance damage) and Nephrotoxicity (usually reversible) — both require monitoring, especially with prolonged therapy.
</details>

## E. Fluoroquinolones

**Classification/Examples:** Ciprofloxacin, Norfloxacin, Ofloxacin, Levofloxacin, Moxifloxacin (newer/"respiratory" quinolones with enhanced Gram +ve/atypical cover).

**Mechanism of action:** Inhibit bacterial DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV → prevent DNA supercoiling/replication — **bactericidal**; broad-spectrum, good oral bioavailability.

**Dose (representative):** Ciprofloxacin — 250–750 mg orally twice daily (or 200–400 mg IV twice daily).

**Indications:** UTI, typhoid fever, respiratory tract infections (esp. Levofloxacin/Moxifloxacin), GI infections, gonorrhoea, anthrax.

**Contraindications/cautions:** **Avoid in children and pregnancy** (risk of cartilage/joint damage in growing individuals), avoid with antacids/dairy (chelation reduces absorption), risk of tendinitis/tendon rupture (esp. elderly, with corticosteroid use), caution in epilepsy (may lower seizure threshold).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why avoid fluoroquinolones in children and pregnant women?</summary>
Risk of arthropathy — damage to growing cartilage/joints, demonstrated in animal studies; therefore avoided in children and during pregnancy unless benefit clearly outweighs risk.
</details>

## F. Macrolides

**Classification/Examples:** Erythromycin (prototype), Azithromycin, Clarithromycin, Roxithromycin.

**Mechanism of action:** Bind to the 50S ribosomal subunit → inhibit bacterial protein synthesis (translocation step) — mainly **bacteriostatic** (can be bactericidal at high concentrations).

**Dose (representative):** Azithromycin — 500 mg orally once daily for 3 days; Erythromycin — 250–500 mg orally every 6 hours.

**Indications:** Alternative to penicillin in penicillin-allergic patients (streptococcal infections), atypical pneumonia (Mycoplasma, Legionella, Chlamydia), whooping cough, gonorrhoea/chlamydial STIs (Azithromycin).

**Contraindications/cautions:** Hepatic impairment (Erythromycin — hepatotoxic potential), GI intolerance (common with Erythromycin), QT prolongation risk — caution with other QT-prolonging drugs; Erythromycin is a CYP3A4 inhibitor (many drug interactions).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why is Azithromycin often preferred over Erythromycin clinically?</summary>
Better GI tolerability, longer half-life allowing once-daily/short-course dosing, and broader tissue penetration — while Erythromycin causes more GI side effects and has more drug interactions (CYP3A4 inhibition).
</details>

## G. Tetracyclines

**Classification/Examples:** Tetracycline, Doxycycline, Minocycline (Doxycycline most commonly used clinically today).

**Mechanism of action:** Bind to the 30S ribosomal subunit → block binding of aminoacyl-tRNA, inhibiting protein synthesis — **bacteriostatic**; broad-spectrum.

**Dose (representative):** Doxycycline — 100 mg orally twice daily on day 1, then once daily; Tetracycline — 250–500 mg orally every 6 hours.

**Indications:** Rickettsial infections, chlamydial infections, Lyme disease, acne vulgaris, cholera, malaria prophylaxis/treatment (as adjunct), brucellosis.

**Contraindications/cautions:** **Avoid in children <8 years and pregnancy** — causes permanent tooth discolouration and inhibits bone growth by chelating calcium; avoid with milk/antacids/iron (chelation reduces absorption); photosensitivity (avoid excess sun exposure).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why are tetracyclines contraindicated in young children and pregnancy?</summary>
They chelate calcium and deposit in developing bones/teeth, causing permanent tooth discolouration and inhibition of bone growth in the fetus/young child.
</details>

## H. Sulphonamides

**Classification/Examples:** Sulfadiazine, Sulfamethoxazole (commonly combined with Trimethoprim as **Co-trimoxazole**), Sulfasalazine (used in IBD), Silver sulfadiazine (topical, burns).

**Structural note (frequently asked as fill-in-the-blank):** Sulphonamides are structurally similar to **Para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA)**.

**Mechanism of action:** Competitive inhibitors of PABA in the folate synthesis pathway — inhibit dihydropteroate synthase → block bacterial folic acid synthesis (bacteria, unlike humans, must synthesize their own folate) — **bacteriostatic**.

**Dose (representative):** Co-trimoxazole (Sulfamethoxazole + Trimethoprim) — 800/160 mg orally twice daily.

**Indications:** UTI, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis/treatment (esp. in HIV), traveller's diarrhoea, toxoplasmosis (Sulfadiazine + Pyrimethamine), burns (Silver sulfadiazine — topical).

**Contraindications/cautions:** Hypersensitivity (Stevens-Johnson syndrome risk), **avoid in pregnancy near term and in newborns** (risk of kernicterus — displaces bilirubin from albumin), G6PD deficiency (haemolysis risk), crystalluria (advise adequate fluid intake).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: What compound do sulphonamides structurally resemble, and why is this significant?</summary>
Para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) — this structural similarity allows sulphonamides to competitively inhibit the bacterial enzyme (dihydropteroate synthase) that uses PABA to synthesize folic acid, which bacteria need for growth.
</details>

## I. Anti-tubercular Drugs

**Classification — First-line vs Second-line (high-yield):**
| Category | Drugs |
|---|---|
| First-line | Rifampicin, Isoniazid, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol, Streptomycin |
| Second-line (drug-resistant TB) | Fluoroquinolones (Levofloxacin), Aminoglycosides (Kanamycin, Amikacin), Ethionamide, Cycloserine, PAS (Para-aminosalicylic acid) |

**Mechanism of action (brief, per drug):**
| Drug | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Rifampicin | Inhibits bacterial DNA-dependent RNA polymerase |
| Isoniazid | Inhibits mycolic acid synthesis (cell wall component) |
| Pyrazinamide | Active in acidic pH of macrophages; exact mechanism not fully clear, disrupts membrane |
| Ethambutol | Inhibits arabinosyl transferase (cell wall arabinogalactan synthesis) |
| Streptomycin | Inhibits protein synthesis (30S ribosomal subunit) |

**Dose (representative, daily adult regimen):** Rifampicin 450–600 mg, Isoniazid 300 mg, Pyrazinamide 1500–2000 mg, Ethambutol 800–1200 mg — all once daily, oral.

**Indications:** Pulmonary and extrapulmonary tuberculosis (always given in combination — **never as monotherapy**, to prevent resistance); Rifampicin also used for leprosy, meningococcal prophylaxis.

**Contraindications/adverse effects (very high-yield — see also Pharmacotherapeutics Ch.2g):**
| Drug | Key ADR |
|---|---|
| Rifampicin | Hepatotoxicity, orange-red body secretions, potent enzyme inducer (many drug interactions) |
| Isoniazid | Peripheral neuropathy (prevented by Pyridoxine/B6), hepatotoxicity |
| Pyrazinamide | Hepatotoxicity, hyperuricaemia |
| Ethambutol | Optic neuritis (red-green colour blindness) — avoid in young children who can't report visual changes |
| Streptomycin | Ototoxicity, nephrotoxicity |

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why must anti-TB drugs always be given in combination, never as monotherapy?</summary>
Monotherapy rapidly selects for drug-resistant mycobacterial mutants; combination therapy with drugs acting by different mechanisms prevents/delays the emergence of resistance and increases cure rates.
</details>
