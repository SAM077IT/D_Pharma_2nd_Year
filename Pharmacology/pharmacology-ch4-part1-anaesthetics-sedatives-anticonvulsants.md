## A. General Anaesthetics

**Definition:** Drugs that produce a reversible loss of all sensation and consciousness, used to allow surgical/other painful procedures to be performed without distress.

**Classification:**
| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Inhalational (gaseous) | Nitrous oxide, Halothane, Isoflurane, Sevoflurane, Desflurane |
| Inhalational (volatile liquid) | Halothane, Isoflurane, Sevoflurane |
| Intravenous | Thiopentone sodium, Propofol, Ketamine, Etomidate |

**Stages of anaesthesia (Guedel's classification — very high-yield, frequently asked as MCQ, divided into 4 stages):**
| Stage | Feature |
|---|---|
| Stage I — Analgesia | Consciousness retained, pain sensation gradually lost |
| Stage II — Excitement/Delirium | Loss of consciousness, involuntary movements, irregular breathing — **most dangerous stage**, laryngospasm risk; goal is to pass through this stage quickly |
| Stage III — Surgical anaesthesia | Regular breathing, loss of reflexes, adequate muscle relaxation — surgery is performed here (divided into 4 planes) |
| Stage IV — Medullary paralysis | Respiratory and vasomotor centre depression — **danger stage**, can be fatal, must be avoided |

**Mechanism of action:** Not fully understood for all agents; generally enhance inhibitory neurotransmission (GABA-A receptor potentiation) and/or inhibit excitatory neurotransmission (NMDA receptor antagonism, e.g., Ketamine) in the CNS.

**Dose (representative):** Thiopentone — 3–5 mg/kg IV (induction); Nitrous oxide — 50–70% inhaled in combination with oxygen (maintenance, as an adjunct — cannot be used alone as it lacks sufficient potency).

**Indications:** Induction and/or maintenance of general anaesthesia for surgery; Ketamine — particularly useful in emergency/field settings and paediatrics (maintains airway reflexes and BP); Nitrous oxide — adjunct for analgesia, labour analgesia (as Entonox).

**Contraindications/cautions:** Halothane — hepatotoxicity risk ("halothane hepatitis") with repeated use, malignant hyperthermia risk (all volatile agents, genetically susceptible individuals); Ketamine — caution in hypertension, raised intracranial pressure (though this view has been revised in recent literature), causes emergence delirium/hallucinations; Nitrous oxide — expands air-filled body cavities (avoid in pneumothorax, bowel obstruction).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield PYQ: What are the advantages and disadvantages of Nitrous oxide?</summary>
**Advantages:** Rapid onset and recovery, good analgesic properties, non-irritant, non-flammable in clinical concentrations, minimal cardiovascular/respiratory depression. **Disadvantages:** Weak anaesthetic potency (cannot produce surgical anaesthesia alone — always combined with other agents), risk of diffusion hypoxia on discontinuation (give 100% oxygen afterward), expands closed air spaces (contraindicated in pneumothorax, intestinal obstruction, middle ear surgery), prolonged use associated with bone marrow suppression/vitamin B12 inactivation.
</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why is Stage II (excitement) considered the most dangerous stage of anaesthesia?</summary>
Reflexes are still active but consciousness is impaired, leading to risk of laryngospasm, vomiting/aspiration, and irregular cardiovascular/respiratory responses — modern IV induction agents aim to move the patient through this stage as rapidly as possible.
</details>

## B. Hypnotics and Sedatives

**Definition:** **Sedatives** reduce anxiety and produce a calming effect without inducing sleep (at low doses); **Hypnotics** produce drowsiness and facilitate the onset/maintenance of sleep (typically higher doses of the same drug class in many cases).

**Classification:**
| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Benzodiazepines | Diazepam, Nitrazepam, Alprazolam, Temazepam |
| Barbiturates (older, less used now due to safety) | Phenobarbitone, Thiopentone (also used as anaesthetic) |
| Newer non-benzodiazepine hypnotics ("Z-drugs") | Zolpidem, Zopiclone, Zaleplon |
| Others | Chloral hydrate, Melatonin receptor agonists (Ramelteon) |

**Mechanism of action:** Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs enhance GABA-A receptor-mediated chloride channel opening (potentiate inhibitory GABA transmission), causing CNS depression. Barbiturates also enhance GABA action but at higher doses can directly open the chloride channel (narrower safety margin, more dangerous in overdose).

**Dose (representative):** Diazepam — 5–10 mg orally at bedtime (hypnotic use) or 2–5 mg 2–3 times/day (anxiolytic use); Zolpidem — 5–10 mg orally at bedtime.

**Indications:** Short-term management of insomnia, preoperative sedation, anxiety, seizures/status epilepticus (Diazepam — IV), alcohol withdrawal (Benzodiazepines), muscle spasm.

**Contraindications/cautions:** Respiratory depression (avoid/caution in COPD, sleep apnoea), risk of tolerance/dependence with prolonged use (especially Benzodiazepines and Barbiturates), avoid abrupt discontinuation after prolonged use (withdrawal seizures), avoid in pregnancy, caution in elderly (falls, confusion), avoid with alcohol/other CNS depressants (additive respiratory depression).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why are benzodiazepines generally preferred over barbiturates as sedative-hypnotics today?</summary>
Benzodiazepines have a much wider margin of safety (less risk of fatal respiratory depression in overdose), lower abuse/dependence potential than barbiturates, and have a specific reversal agent (Flumazenil) — barbiturates have no specific antidote and a narrow therapeutic index.
</details>

## C. Anti-Convulsant (Anti-epileptic) Drugs

**Definition:** Drugs used to prevent or control seizures in epilepsy, by reducing abnormal, excessive neuronal excitability.

**Classification (by mechanism — high-yield):**
| Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|
| Sodium channel blockers | **Phenytoin** (Diphenylhydantoin), Carbamazepine, Lamotrigine |
| GABA enhancers | Phenobarbitone, Benzodiazepines (Diazepam, Clonazepam) |
| Calcium channel modulators (T-type, thalamic) | Ethosuximide (absence seizures) |
| Multiple mechanisms | Sodium valproate (Na+ channel + GABA enhancement) |
| Newer agents | Levetiracetam, Gabapentin, Topiramate |

**Mechanism of action (representative):**
- **Phenytoin:** Blocks voltage-gated sodium channels, stabilizing neuronal membranes and preventing repetitive firing
- **Sodium valproate:** Increases GABA levels and also blocks sodium channels
- **Ethosuximide:** Blocks T-type calcium channels in thalamic neurons — specific for absence seizures

**Dose (representative):** Phenytoin — 100 mg orally 2–3 times daily (adjusted to serum levels, narrow therapeutic range 10–20 mcg/mL); Sodium valproate — 15–60 mg/kg/day in divided doses.

**Indications (by seizure type — see also Pharmacotherapeutics Ch.2d for detailed clinical mapping):** Generalized tonic-clonic — Phenytoin, Sodium valproate, Carbamazepine; Absence seizures — Ethosuximide, Sodium valproate; Focal seizures — Carbamazepine, Phenytoin; Status epilepticus — IV Diazepam/Lorazepam followed by Phenytoin loading.

**Contraindications/cautions:** Phenytoin — gingival hyperplasia, hirsutism, ataxia/nystagmus at toxic levels, teratogenic (avoid in pregnancy where possible); Sodium valproate — hepatotoxicity, teratogenic (neural tube defects — strongly avoided in pregnancy); Carbamazepine — aplastic anaemia/agranulocytosis (rare), enzyme inducer (many interactions); all anti-epileptics — avoid abrupt withdrawal (can precipitate seizures/status epilepticus).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield PYQ: Diphenylhydantoin is used in the treatment of ___________.</summary>
Epilepsy (Diphenylhydantoin is the older chemical name for Phenytoin) — specifically generalized tonic-clonic and focal seizures.
</details>

## D. Anti-Anxiety (Anxiolytic) Drugs

**Definition:** Drugs used to reduce symptoms of anxiety without significant sedation at therapeutic doses (ideally).

**Classification:**
| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Benzodiazepines | Diazepam, Alprazolam, Lorazepam, Chlordiazepoxide |
| Azapirones | Buspirone |
| SSRIs (used for long-term anxiety management, see Part 2) | Sertraline, Escitalopram |
| Beta-blockers (for somatic symptoms) | Propranolol |

**Mechanism of action:** Benzodiazepines — enhance GABA-A receptor-mediated inhibitory neurotransmission (as above). Buspirone — partial agonist at serotonin 5-HT1A receptors, mechanism distinct from GABA system, non-sedating, no dependence potential, but slower onset (takes 2–4 weeks for full effect).

**Dose (representative):** Diazepam — 2–10 mg orally 2–4 times daily (short-term use); Buspirone — 5 mg orally 2–3 times daily, increased gradually.

**Indications:** Generalized anxiety disorder, short-term relief of severe anxiety, panic attacks (acute — Benzodiazepines), preoperative anxiety, alcohol withdrawal, performance anxiety (Propranolol — somatic symptoms like tremor/palpitations).

**Contraindications/cautions:** Benzodiazepines — risk of tolerance/dependence with prolonged use (restrict to short-term use), sedation/impaired coordination (avoid driving), avoid in pregnancy and with alcohol; Buspirone — does not work for acute anxiety/panic attacks (too slow onset), but has no sedation/dependence liability — useful for chronic anxiety management.

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why is Buspirone not useful for an acute panic attack despite being an anxiolytic?</summary>
It has a delayed onset of action (taking 2–4 weeks for full therapeutic effect via 5-HT1A partial agonism), unlike benzodiazepines which act within minutes — so it is suited only to chronic/generalized anxiety management, not acute symptom relief.
</details>

## Quick Revision Table

| Drug class | Prototype | Key mechanism | Key caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| General anaesthetics | Thiopentone/Nitrous oxide | GABA-A potentiation / NMDA antagonism (Ketamine) | Stage II = most dangerous; N2O — diffusion hypoxia |
