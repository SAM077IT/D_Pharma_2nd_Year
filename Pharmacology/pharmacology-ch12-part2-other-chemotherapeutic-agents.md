## A. Anti-fungal Drugs

**Classification (by chemical group — high-yield):**
| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Polyenes | Amphotericin B, Nystatin |
| Azoles | Fluconazole, Ketoconazole, Itraconazole, Clotrimazole (topical) |
| Allylamines | Terbinafine |
| Echinocandins | Caspofungin |
| Antimetabolite | Flucytosine (5-FC) |

**Mechanism of action:**
| Group | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Polyenes | Bind ergosterol in fungal cell membrane → pore formation → leakage of cell contents (fungicidal) |
| Azoles | Inhibit ergosterol synthesis (via CYP-dependent 14-α-demethylase) → damaged membrane (fungistatic) |
| Allylamines | Inhibit squalene epoxidase (an earlier step in ergosterol synthesis) |
| Echinocandins | Inhibit fungal cell wall (β-glucan) synthesis |

**Dose (representative):** Fluconazole — 150 mg single dose (vaginal candidiasis) or 200–400 mg/day (systemic infections); Amphotericin B — 0.5–1 mg/kg/day IV (slow infusion).

**Indications:** Amphotericin B — systemic/deep mycoses (drug of choice for many severe fungal infections despite toxicity); Fluconazole — candidiasis, cryptococcal meningitis; Clotrimazole/Terbinafine — topical dermatophyte/ringworm infections.

**Contraindications/cautions:** Amphotericin B — significant **nephrotoxicity**, infusion-related fever/chills ("shake and bake" reaction), hypokalaemia; Azoles — hepatotoxicity, multiple CYP450 drug interactions, avoid Ketoconazole in pregnancy.

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why does Amphotericin B have significant toxicity despite being highly effective?</summary>
Fungal and human cells both use sterols in their membranes (ergosterol in fungi, cholesterol in humans); Amphotericin B has some affinity for cholesterol too, causing toxicity (notably nephrotoxicity) — a classic example of imperfect selective toxicity.
</details>

## B. Anti-viral Drugs

**Classification (by target virus/mechanism — high-yield, was directly asked as "Classify anti-viral drugs" in a previous exam):**
| Category | Examples | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| Anti-herpes agents | Acyclovir, Valacyclovir | HSV, VZV (chickenpox/shingles) |
| Anti-influenza agents | Oseltamivir, Zanamivir (neuraminidase inhibitors) | Influenza A & B |
| Anti-retroviral (HIV) agents | Zidovudine (NRTI), Nevirapine (NNRTI), Ritonavir (PI), Dolutegravir (INSTI) | HIV/AIDS |
| Anti-hepatitis agents | Tenofovir, Entecavir (Hep B); Sofosbuvir (Hep C) | Viral hepatitis |
| Broad-spectrum antiviral | Ribavirin | RSV, Hepatitis C (combination) |

**Mechanism of action (representative):**
- **Acyclovir:** Guanosine analogue — activated by viral thymidine kinase, incorporated into viral DNA, terminates DNA chain synthesis (highly selective for infected cells)
- **Oseltamivir:** Inhibits viral neuraminidase, preventing release of new virions from infected cells
- **Zidovudine:** Nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor — blocks HIV reverse transcriptase, halting viral DNA synthesis

**Dose (representative):** Acyclovir — 200–400 mg orally 5 times/day (or IV in severe infection); Oseltamivir — 75 mg orally twice daily for 5 days.

**Indications:** Acyclovir — genital herpes, herpes zoster, chickenpox; Oseltamivir — influenza (started within 48 hrs of symptom onset for best effect); ART combinations — chronic HIV management.

**Contraindications/cautions:** Acyclovir — dose adjustment in renal impairment (crystalluria risk — ensure hydration); Zidovudine — bone marrow suppression (anaemia); general caution — antivirals are largely virustatic (suppress replication) rather than curative for chronic viral infections.

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why is Acyclovir selectively toxic to virus-infected cells only?</summary>
It requires activation (phosphorylation) by viral thymidine kinase, an enzyme present in herpes-infected cells but not efficiently in normal host cells — so the active drug accumulates selectively where the virus is replicating.
</details>

## C. Anti-amoebic Agents

**Definition:** Drugs used to treat amoebiasis, caused by *Entamoeba histolytica*.

**Classification (by site of action — high-yield):**
| Type | Examples | Site of action |
|---|---|---|
| Tissue amoebicides (systemic) | **Metronidazole**, Tinidazole | Act on amoebae in intestinal wall and liver (invasive disease) |
| Luminal amoebicides | Diloxanide furoate, Paromomycin | Act only on amoebae in the intestinal lumen (used after tissue amoebicide to eradicate cysts/prevent relapse) |
| Mixed amoebicides | Chloroquine (for hepatic amoebiasis, adjunct) | Both luminal & tissue (variably) |

**Mechanism of action (Metronidazole):** Undergoes reduction inside the anaerobic organism → forms cytotoxic free radicals/intermediates that damage microbial DNA — active only under anaerobic conditions (which is why it's specific to anaerobes/protozoa).

**Dose (representative):** Metronidazole — 400–800 mg orally three times daily for 5–10 days (invasive amoebiasis); Diloxanide furoate — 500 mg orally three times daily for 10 days (luminal clearance, given after Metronidazole).

**Indications:** Intestinal amoebiasis (amoebic dysentery), amoebic liver abscess, also used for giardiasis and anaerobic bacterial infections/trichomoniasis.

**Contraindications/cautions:** Avoid alcohol during and for 48 hrs after Metronidazole (**disulfiram-like reaction** — nausea, flushing, palpitations), caution in first trimester of pregnancy, metallic taste is a common side effect.

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why is Metronidazole always followed by a luminal amoebicide in invasive amoebiasis?</summary>
Metronidazole (a tissue amoebicide) does not reliably eliminate amoebae/cysts residing in the intestinal lumen; a luminal agent like Diloxanide furoate is needed afterward to prevent relapse and further transmission.
</details>

## D. Anthelmintics

**Classification (by drug — commonly asked to classify by target worm, high-yield):**
| Drug | Spectrum/Use |
|---|---|
| **Albendazole** | Broad-spectrum — roundworms (Ascaris), hookworm, pinworm, whipworm, tapeworm, also neurocysticercosis, hydatid disease |
| Mebendazole | Similar broad-spectrum, mainly intestinal nematodes |
| Pyrantel pamoate | Roundworm, pinworm, hookworm |
| Diethylcarbamazine (DEC) | Filariasis (lymphatic filariasis, drug of choice) |
| Praziquantel | Tapeworms (cestodes), Schistosomiasis (flukes) |
| Ivermectin | Strongyloidiasis, onchocerciasis (river blindness), scabies |
| Niclosamide | Tapeworm infections |

**Mechanism of action (representative):**
- **Albendazole/Mebendazole:** Inhibit microtubule (tubulin) polymerization in the parasite → impaired glucose uptake and eventual worm death
- **Pyrantel pamoate:** Depolarizing neuromuscular blocker → spastic paralysis of the worm (expelled in stool)
- **Diethylcarbamazine:** Immobilizes microfilariae and alters their surface, making them susceptible to host immune destruction
- **Praziquantel:** Increases cell membrane permeability to calcium in the worm → paralysis and dislodgement

**Dose (representative):** Albendazole — 400 mg orally single dose (for most intestinal worms); DEC — 6 mg/kg/day in divided doses for filariasis.

**Indications:** See classification table above — matched by worm type.

**Contraindications/cautions:** Albendazole/Mebendazole — avoid in first trimester of pregnancy (teratogenic potential); DEC — can cause a severe allergic (Mazzotti) reaction in patients with onchocerciasis due to rapid microfilarial death — give with antihistamines/steroids as cover.

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield PYQ: What class of drug is Albendazole?</summary>
Anthelmintic (broad-spectrum) — acts by inhibiting microtubule formation in the parasite.
</details>

## E. Anti-malarial Agents

**Classification (by stage of action — very high-yield, "descriptive note on anti-malarial agents" was a previous exam question):**
| Category | Examples | Acts on |
|---|---|---|
| Tissue schizonticides (causal prophylactic) | Primaquine | Pre-erythrocytic (liver) stage |
| Blood schizonticides | Chloroquine, Artemisinin derivatives (Artesunate), Quinine, Mefloquine | Erythrocytic (blood) stage — used for acute attack/cure |
| Hypnozoiticides (radical cure) | Primaquine | Dormant liver hypnozoites (*P. vivax*/*P. ovale* relapse prevention) |
| Gametocytocides | Primaquine, Artemisinin derivatives | Gametocyte stage (prevents transmission to mosquito) |

**Mechanism of action (representative):**
- **Chloroquine:** Concentrates in the parasite's food vacuole, inhibits haem polymerase → toxic haem accumulates, killing the parasite
- **Artemisinin derivatives:** Generate free radicals via cleavage of an endoperoxide bridge, damaging parasite proteins/membranes — fastest-acting antimalarials
- **Primaquine:** Mechanism not fully clear; generates oxidative metabolites toxic to the parasite in liver stages

**Dose (representative):** Chloroquine — 25 mg/kg total dose over 3 days for *P. vivax*; Artemisinin Combination Therapy (ACT) for *P. falciparum* — e.g., Artesunate + Sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine as per national guidelines.

**Indications:** Treatment and chemoprophylaxis of malaria (species- and resistance-pattern dependent — see Pharmacotherapeutics Ch.2g for clinical management approach).

**Contraindications/cautions:** Primaquine — **check G6PD status first** (risk of severe haemolysis in deficiency), avoid in pregnancy; Chloroquine — retinal toxicity with prolonged use, caution in psoriasis/epilepsy; Quinine — cinchonism (tinnitus, headache, visual disturbance), hypoglycaemia.

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why is Primaquine essential for radical cure of P. vivax, and what must be checked before giving it?</summary>
It is the only drug that eliminates dormant liver hypnozoites of *P. vivax*/*P. ovale*, preventing relapse. G6PD status must be checked first, since Primaquine can cause severe haemolysis in G6PD-deficient patients.
</details>

## F. Anti-neoplastic Agents

**Classification (by mechanism — very high-yield, "Define anti-neoplastic drugs, classify according to mechanism" is a recurring exam question):**
| Class | Examples | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Alkylating agents | Cyclophosphamide, Chlorambucil, Busulfan | Cross-link DNA strands, preventing replication |
| Antimetabolites | Methotrexate, 5-Fluorouracil, 6-Mercaptopurine | Structural analogues of normal metabolites, block DNA/RNA synthesis |
| Antitumour antibiotics | Doxorubicin, Bleomycin, Dactinomycin | Intercalate DNA / cause strand breaks |
| Plant alkaloids (mitotic inhibitors) | Vincristine, Vinblastine (vinca alkaloids); Paclitaxel (taxane) | Disrupt microtubule function → arrest mitosis |
| Hormonal agents | Tamoxifen (anti-oestrogen), Prednisolone | Modify hormone-dependent tumour growth |
| Topoisomerase inhibitors | Etoposide, Irinotecan | Inhibit DNA topoisomerase enzymes, causing DNA strand breaks |
| Targeted therapy/Biologic agents | Imatinib (tyrosine kinase inhibitor), Trastuzumab (monoclonal antibody) | Target specific molecular pathways in cancer cells |

**General principle:** Most conventional cytotoxic anti-neoplastic drugs act on **rapidly dividing cells** (a feature of cancer cells) — this is also why they damage normal rapidly-dividing tissues (bone marrow, GI mucosa, hair follicles), explaining common toxicities.

**Dose:** Highly individualized based on body surface area, cancer type/stage, and combination protocol — not typically expected as a fixed number in D.Pharm exams (focus is on classification/mechanism/toxicity instead).

**Indications:** Various — matched to specific cancer types/protocols (combination chemotherapy is standard, exploiting different mechanisms to increase efficacy and reduce resistance).

**Common adverse effects (high-yield, "indications of biological agents" and general antineoplastic toxicity are common short-note questions):**
| Toxicity | Cause |
|---|---|
| Myelosuppression | Damage to rapidly dividing bone marrow cells → anaemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia |
| Nausea/vomiting | Direct stimulation of the chemoreceptor trigger zone |
| Alopecia | Damage to rapidly dividing hair follicle cells |
| Mucositis/GI toxicity | Damage to rapidly dividing GI mucosal cells |
| Cardiotoxicity | Specific to certain agents, e.g., Doxorubicin (dose-dependent) |
| Nephrotoxicity | Specific to certain agents, e.g., Cisplatin |

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why do anti-neoplastic drugs commonly cause bone marrow suppression, hair loss, and GI mucositis?</summary>
Most cytotoxic anti-neoplastic drugs cannot fully distinguish cancer cells from other rapidly dividing normal cells (bone marrow, hair follicles, GI mucosa) — these normal tissues are collateral targets of the same mechanism that kills cancer cells.
</details>
