# Pharmacotherapeutics — Chapter 2(g)
## Infectious Diseases: TB · Pneumonia · UTI · Hepatitis · Gonorrhoea & Syphilis · Malaria · HIV · Viral Infections (SARS-CoV-2)

> **Weightage:** 12 hrs (highest single sub-topic) · **3-day plan:** Day 2

---

## 1. Tuberculosis (TB)

**Definition:** Chronic granulomatous infectious disease caused by *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, primarily affecting the lungs.

**Clinical manifestations:** Chronic cough (>2 weeks) ± haemoptysis, evening pyrexia, night sweats, weight loss, anorexia.

**Diagnosis:** Sputum AFB smear/CBNAAT (GeneXpert), chest X-ray, Mantoux test.

**Non-pharmacological management:** DOTS (Directly Observed Treatment, Short-course), nutritional support, contact tracing/screening, patient isolation during infectious period, treatment adherence counselling.

**Pharmacological management — mnemonic "RIPE":**
| Drug | Key ADR |
|---|---|
| **R**ifampicin | Orange-red body secretions, hepatotoxicity, enzyme inducer |
| **I**soniazid | Peripheral neuropathy (prevent with **Pyridoxine/B6**), hepatotoxicity |
| **P**yrazinamide | Hepatotoxicity, hyperuricaemia |
| **E**thambutol | Optic neuritis (visual disturbance) |

**Standard regimen:** Intensive phase — RIPE for 2 months; Continuation phase — Rifampicin + Isoniazid for 4 months (2RIPE/4RI, per RNTCP/NTEP India).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why give Pyridoxine (Vitamin B6) with Isoniazid?</summary>
Prevents Isoniazid-induced peripheral neuropathy (INH depletes pyridoxine).
</details>

## 2. Pneumonia

**Definition:** Infection/inflammation of lung parenchyma (alveoli), caused by bacteria, viruses, or fungi.

**Classification:** Community-acquired (CAP — most common: *S. pneumoniae*) vs Hospital-acquired (HAP).

**Clinical manifestations:** Fever, productive cough, pleuritic chest pain, dyspnoea, tachypnoea; crepitations/bronchial breathing on auscultation.

**Non-pharmacological management:** Adequate hydration, rest, oxygen therapy if hypoxic, chest physiotherapy, vaccination (pneumococcal, influenza) for prevention.

**Pharmacological management:**
| Setting | Empirical therapy |
|---|---|
| Outpatient CAP (no comorbidity) | Amoxicillin or Macrolide (Azithromycin) |
| Inpatient CAP | Beta-lactam (Ceftriaxone) + Macrolide |
| Severe/ICU | Beta-lactam + Fluoroquinolone (Levofloxacin) or Macrolide |

## 3. Urinary Tract Infection (UTI)

**Definition:** Infection of any part of the urinary tract (cystitis — lower; pyelonephritis — upper); most commonly caused by *E. coli*.

**Clinical manifestations:** Dysuria, urinary frequency/urgency, suprapubic pain (cystitis); fever, flank pain, chills, nausea (pyelonephritis — upper tract involvement).

**Non-pharmacological management:** Increased fluid intake, proper perineal hygiene, voiding after intercourse, avoid holding urine.

**Pharmacological management:**
| Type | 1st-line |
|---|---|
| Uncomplicated cystitis | Nitrofurantoin, Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (Co-trimoxazole), Fosfomycin |
| Pyelonephritis | Fluoroquinolones (Ciprofloxacin) or IV Ceftriaxone if severe |

## 4. Viral Hepatitis

**Definition:** Inflammation of the liver caused by hepatotropic viruses (A–E).

| Type | Transmission | Chronicity |
|---|---|---|
| Hepatitis A | Faeco-oral | Self-limiting, no chronic state |
| Hepatitis B | Blood/body fluids, vertical | Can become chronic |
| Hepatitis C | Blood (IV drug use, transfusion) | Frequently becomes chronic |
| Hepatitis E | Faeco-oral | Self-limiting (severe in pregnancy) |

**Clinical manifestations:** Jaundice, anorexia, malaise, right upper quadrant pain, dark urine, pale stools, hepatomegaly.

**Non-pharmacological management:** Rest, adequate nutrition/hydration, avoid alcohol and hepatotoxic drugs, hepatitis A/B vaccination for prevention.

**Pharmacological management:**
| Type | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Hepatitis A/E | Supportive only (self-limiting) |
| Hepatitis B (chronic) | Tenofovir, Entecavir |
| Hepatitis C | Direct-acting antivirals — Sofosbuvir + Daclatasvir/Velpatasvir |

## 5. Gonorrhoea and Syphilis (STIs)

**Gonorrhoea — Definition:** STI caused by *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*. **Clinical features:** Urethral discharge, dysuria (males); often asymptomatic or cervicitis/PID (females). **Treatment:** Ceftriaxone (IM, single dose) ± Azithromycin (cover possible co-infection with Chlamydia).

**Syphilis — Definition:** STI caused by *Treponema pallidum*, spirochete bacterium.

| Stage | Feature |
|---|---|
| Primary | Painless genital ulcer (chancre) |
| Secondary | Rash (palms/soles), lymphadenopathy, condylomata lata |
| Latent | Asymptomatic, serology positive |
| Tertiary | Gummas, cardiovascular/neurosyphilis (years later) |

**Treatment:** **Benzathine Penicillin G (IM)** — drug of choice at all stages (dosing/duration varies by stage); Doxycycline if penicillin-allergic.

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction</summary>
Acute febrile reaction (fever, chills, myalgia) that can occur within hours of starting antibiotic treatment for syphilis, due to rapid killing of spirochetes releasing endotoxin-like substances — self-limiting, managed with antipyretics.
</details>

## 6. Malaria

**Definition:** Mosquito-borne (female *Anopheles*) parasitic infection caused by *Plasmodium* species (*P. vivax*, *P. falciparum* most relevant in India; *falciparum* causes severe/complicated malaria).

**Clinical manifestations:** Cyclical fever with chills and rigors, headache, myalgia, splenomegaly; *P. falciparum* — risk of cerebral malaria, severe anaemia, renal failure (complicated malaria).

**Non-pharmacological management:** Vector control (mosquito nets, indoor residual spraying), avoiding stagnant water, chemoprophylaxis for travelers to endemic areas.

**Pharmacological management:**
| Species | Treatment |
|---|---|
| *P. vivax* | Chloroquine + Primaquine (Primaquine for radical cure — kills hypnozoites in liver) |
| *P. falciparum* (uncomplicated) | Artemisinin-based Combination Therapy (ACT) — e.g., Artesunate + Sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine |
| Severe/complicated malaria | IV Artesunate |

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why is Primaquine essential in P. vivax treatment?</summary>
*P. vivax* forms dormant liver hypnozoites causing relapse; Primaquine provides "radical cure" by eradicating these liver stages (check G6PD status first — risk of haemolysis in deficiency).
</details>

## 7. HIV and Opportunistic Infections

**Definition:** Chronic viral infection by Human Immunodeficiency Virus, progressively destroying CD4+ T-lymphocytes, leading to AIDS (Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome) with opportunistic infections.

**Clinical manifestations:** Acute retroviral syndrome (flu-like, early) → asymptomatic latency → AIDS-defining illnesses (CD4 <200 cells/mm³) — Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, Tuberculosis, Candidiasis, Toxoplasmosis, Kaposi's sarcoma.

**Non-pharmacological management:** Safe sex practices, needle-exchange programs, prevention of mother-to-child transmission, nutritional support, adherence counselling.

**Pharmacological management — ART (Antiretroviral Therapy):** Combination of ≥3 drugs from different classes (to prevent resistance):
| Class | Examples |
|---|---|
| NRTIs | Tenofovir, Lamivudine, Zidovudine |
| NNRTIs | Efavirenz, Nevirapine |
| Protease inhibitors | Ritonavir, Lopinavir |
| Integrase inhibitors | Dolutegravir |

**Opportunistic infection prophylaxis:** Co-trimoxazole (PCP prophylaxis when CD4 low).

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Standard first-line ART regimen concept</summary>
Typically 2 NRTIs + 1 Integrase inhibitor (e.g., Tenofovir + Lamivudine + Dolutegravir) — current WHO-preferred first-line regimen.
</details>

## 8. Viral Infections — SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19)

**Definition:** Respiratory illness caused by SARS-CoV-2 coronavirus, ranging from asymptomatic/mild to severe pneumonia and ARDS.

**Clinical manifestations:** Fever, cough, loss of taste/smell (anosmia/ageusia — characteristic), sore throat, fatigue, dyspnoea in moderate-severe disease.

**Non-pharmacological management:** Isolation, vaccination, hand hygiene, masking, supportive care (rest, hydration, antipyretics) for mild cases; oxygen therapy/proning for hypoxic patients.

**Pharmacological management:**
| Severity | Treatment concept |
|---|---|
| Mild | Symptomatic (antipyretics); usually no antivirals needed |
| Moderate-severe (hospitalized, hypoxic) | Remdesivir (antiviral), Dexamethasone (reduces mortality in hypoxic patients) |
| Severe/cytokine storm | Tocilizumab (IL-6 inhibitor) in selected cases |

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why is Dexamethasone beneficial only in hypoxic/severe COVID-19, not mild disease?</summary>
Steroids suppress the excessive inflammatory response driving lung damage in severe disease; in mild disease (viral replication phase, no significant inflammation yet), steroids can be harmful by suppressing the immune response needed to clear the virus.
</details>

## Quick Revision Table

| Disease | Causative agent | 1st-line treatment |
|---|---|---|
| TB | *M. tuberculosis* | RIPE regimen (2 months) + RI (4 months) |
| CAP pneumonia | *S. pneumoniae* (most common) | Amoxicillin/Macrolide |
| UTI (cystitis) | *E. coli* | Nitrofurantoin/Co-trimoxazole |
| Hepatitis B (chronic) | HBV | Tenofovir/Entecavir |
| Gonorrhoea | *N. gonorrhoeae* | Ceftriaxone (IM) |
| Syphilis | *T. pallidum* | Benzathine Penicillin G |
| Malaria (falciparum) | *Plasmodium falciparum* | ACT (Artesunate-based) |
| HIV | HIV virus | ART (2 NRTI + Integrase inhibitor) |
| COVID-19 (severe) | SARS-CoV-2 | Remdesivir + Dexamethasone |

## Self-Test

<details>
<summary>Q1. Expand RIPE and name the ADR most associated with each drug.</summary>
Rifampicin (orange secretions, hepatotoxicity), Isoniazid (peripheral neuropathy), Pyrazinamide (hyperuricaemia), Ethambutol (optic neuritis).
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q2. Why is Pyridoxine given with Isoniazid?</summary>
To prevent Isoniazid-induced peripheral neuropathy.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q3. What is the drug of choice for all stages of syphilis?</summary>
Benzathine Penicillin G (IM).
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q4. Why must G6PD status be checked before giving Primaquine?</summary>
Primaquine can cause severe haemolysis in G6PD-deficient patients.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q5. Name the current WHO-preferred first-line ART combination.</summary>
Tenofovir + Lamivudine + Dolutegravir (2 NRTIs + Integrase inhibitor).
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q6. In COVID-19, when is Dexamethasone indicated and why not in mild disease?</summary>
Indicated in hypoxic/severe disease to reduce mortality by dampening excess inflammation; avoided in mild disease as it may suppress viral clearance during the early replication phase.
</details>
