# Pharmacotherapeutics — Chapter 2(j)
## Psychiatric Disorders: Depression · Anxiety · Psychosis

> **Weightage:** 4 hrs · **3-day plan:** Day 3

---

## 1. Depression (Major Depressive Disorder)

**Definition:** Mood disorder characterized by persistent low mood and/or loss of interest/pleasure (anhedonia), lasting ≥2 weeks, with functional impairment.

**Etiopathogenesis:** Monoamine hypothesis — deficiency of serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine at synapses; genetic predisposition, stressful life events, neuroendocrine dysregulation (HPA axis).

**Clinical manifestations:** Persistent sadness, anhedonia, sleep disturbance (insomnia/hypersomnia), appetite/weight change, fatigue, poor concentration, feelings of worthlessness/guilt, psychomotor changes, suicidal ideation (**always assess risk**).

**Non-pharmacological management:** Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT), psychotherapy, regular exercise, sleep hygiene, social support, in severe/refractory cases — Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT).

**Pharmacological management:**
| Class | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| SSRIs | **Fluoxetine, Sertraline, Escitalopram** | 1st line — better tolerated than older classes |
| SNRIs | Venlafaxine, Duloxetine | Alternative first-line |
| TCAs | Amitriptyline, Imipramine | Effective but more ADRs (anticholinergic, cardiotoxic in overdose) — not first-line |
| MAOIs | Phenelzine | Reserved — dietary tyramine restriction needed (hypertensive crisis risk) |
| Atypical | Mirtazapine, Bupropion | Alternative options based on side-effect profile |

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why do antidepressants take 2–4 weeks to show clinical effect?</summary>
Although neurotransmitter levels rise within hours/days, therapeutic benefit requires downstream adaptive changes — receptor downregulation and neuroplastic changes — which take weeks to develop.
</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Key counselling point for SSRIs regarding suicide risk</summary>
Close monitoring is needed in the first few weeks of treatment, especially in adolescents/young adults, as energy/motivation may improve before mood fully lifts — theoretically increasing capacity to act on pre-existing suicidal ideation.
</details>

## 2. Anxiety Disorders

**Definition:** Excessive, persistent fear/worry disproportionate to actual threat, causing significant distress or functional impairment (e.g., Generalized Anxiety Disorder, Panic Disorder).

**Etiopathogenesis:** Dysregulation of GABA, serotonin, norepinephrine systems; genetic predisposition; environmental stressors/trauma.

**Clinical manifestations:** Excessive worry, restlessness, muscle tension, fatigue, poor concentration, sleep disturbance; panic attacks — sudden intense fear with palpitations, sweating, chest tightness, dyspnoea, fear of dying.

**Non-pharmacological management:** CBT (highly effective), relaxation techniques, regular exercise, avoiding caffeine/stimulants, sleep hygiene.

**Pharmacological management:**
| Class | Examples | Role |
|---|---|---|
| SSRIs/SNRIs | Sertraline, Escitalopram, Venlafaxine | 1st line for long-term management |
| Benzodiazepines | Alprazolam, Diazepam, Lorazepam | Short-term/acute relief only — risk of dependence, avoid long-term use |
| Buspirone | — | Non-sedating alternative, no dependence risk, slower onset |
| Beta-blockers | Propranolol | Useful for somatic symptoms (tremor, palpitations) — e.g., performance anxiety |

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why are benzodiazepines restricted to short-term use in anxiety?</summary>
Risk of tolerance, physical dependence, and withdrawal (including rebound anxiety/seizures on abrupt discontinuation); SSRIs/SNRIs are preferred for long-term maintenance.
</details>

## 3. Psychosis (Schizophrenia)

**Definition:** Severe mental disorder characterized by a break from reality — hallucinations, delusions, and disorganized thinking/behaviour.

**Etiopathogenesis:** Dopamine hypothesis — hyperactivity of dopaminergic pathways (mesolimbic — positive symptoms); genetic predisposition; neurodevelopmental factors.

**Clinical manifestations (positive vs negative symptoms — exam favorite):**
| Positive symptoms | Negative symptoms |
|---|---|
| Hallucinations (commonly auditory) | Flat affect |
| Delusions | Social withdrawal |
| Disorganized speech/behaviour | Anhedonia |
| — | Poverty of speech (alogia), avolition |

**Non-pharmacological management:** Psychosocial rehabilitation, family psychoeducation, social skills training, supported employment, adherence support (long-acting injectable options aid this).

**Pharmacological management:**
| Class | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Typical (1st-gen) antipsychotics | Haloperidol, Chlorpromazine | Effective for positive symptoms; higher risk of extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS) |
| Atypical (2nd-gen) antipsychotics | **Risperidone, Olanzapine, Clozapine** | 1st line generally — lower EPS risk, help negative symptoms too; metabolic ADRs (weight gain, dyslipidaemia, diabetes risk) |
| Clozapine | — | Reserved for treatment-resistant schizophrenia; requires regular WBC monitoring |

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why is regular blood monitoring mandatory with Clozapine?</summary>
Risk of agranulocytosis — regular complete blood counts (weekly initially, then periodically) are mandatory throughout treatment.
</details>

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: What is extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS) and which class of antipsychotics is most associated with it?</summary>
EPS includes acute dystonia, akathisia, Parkinsonism-like symptoms, and tardive dyskinesia (long-term) — caused by excessive dopamine (D2) receptor blockade in the nigrostriatal pathway; more common with typical (1st-generation) antipsychotics like Haloperidol.
</details>

## Quick Revision Table

| Disorder | Key pathophysiology | 1st-line drug |
|---|---|---|
| Depression | ↓ Serotonin/NE/dopamine | SSRIs |
| Anxiety | GABA/serotonin dysregulation | SSRIs/SNRIs (long-term); Benzodiazepines (short-term only) |
| Psychosis | ↑ Mesolimbic dopamine | Atypical antipsychotics |

## Self-Test

<details>
<summary>Q1. Why do antidepressants take weeks to show clinical improvement despite rapid rise in neurotransmitter levels?</summary>
Therapeutic effect depends on slower downstream receptor/neuroplastic adaptations, not the initial rise in neurotransmitter concentration.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q2. Why should benzodiazepines not be used long-term for anxiety?</summary>
Risk of tolerance, dependence, and withdrawal symptoms including rebound anxiety.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q3. Differentiate positive and negative symptoms of schizophrenia with one example each.</summary>
Positive — hallucinations/delusions (symptoms added to normal experience). Negative — flat affect/social withdrawal (normal functions diminished).
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q4. Why is Clozapine reserved for treatment-resistant cases despite being effective?</summary>
Risk of agranulocytosis requiring regular mandatory blood count monitoring.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q5. Which antipsychotic generation carries higher risk of extrapyramidal symptoms?</summary>
Typical/1st-generation antipsychotics (e.g., Haloperidol) — due to more potent D2 receptor blockade.
</details>
