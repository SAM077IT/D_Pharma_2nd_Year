## 1. Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS)

**Definition:** Common endocrine disorder in women of reproductive age, characterized by hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovaries.

**Diagnosis — Rotterdam criteria (any 2 of 3):**
1. Oligo/anovulation
2. Clinical/biochemical hyperandrogenism
3. Polycystic ovaries on ultrasound

**Etiopathogenesis:** Insulin resistance (central feature) → compensatory hyperinsulinaemia → increased ovarian androgen production; genetic predisposition; disrupted GnRH/LH pulsatility.

**Clinical manifestations:** Irregular/absent menses, hirsutism, acne, obesity/weight gain, infertility, acanthosis nigricans (marker of insulin resistance); long-term risk — type 2 diabetes, dyslipidaemia, endometrial hyperplasia/cancer (from unopposed oestrogen).

**Non-pharmacological management:** Weight loss (**first-line intervention** — even 5–10% loss improves ovulation/symptoms), regular exercise, low-glycaemic-index diet.

**Pharmacological management (goal-directed):**
| Goal | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Menstrual regulation/hirsutism/acne (not seeking pregnancy) | Combined oral contraceptives (COCs) |
| Insulin resistance | Metformin |
| Ovulation induction (seeking pregnancy) | Clomiphene citrate or Letrozole (first-line) |
| Hirsutism (add-on) | Spironolactone (anti-androgen) |

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why is Metformin used in PCOS even in non-diabetic patients?</summary>
Insulin resistance is central to PCOS pathophysiology; Metformin improves insulin sensitivity, which can restore ovulatory cycles and reduce hyperandrogenism, independent of a formal diabetes diagnosis.
</details>

## 2. Dysmenorrhea

**Definition:** Painful menstrual cramps of uterine origin, occurring just before/during menstruation.

| Type | Cause |
|---|---|
| Primary | No underlying pelvic pathology — due to excess prostaglandin (PGF2α) release causing uterine contraction/ischaemia |
| Secondary | Underlying pathology — endometriosis, fibroids, pelvic inflammatory disease |

**Clinical manifestations:** Cramping lower abdominal pain radiating to back/thighs, onset with or just before menses, may accompany nausea, fatigue, headache; primary type typically begins in adolescence.

**Non-pharmacological management:** Local heat application, regular exercise, adequate rest, relaxation techniques.

**Pharmacological management:**
| Class | Examples | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| NSAIDs | **Ibuprofen, Mefenamic acid, Naproxen** | 1st line — inhibit prostaglandin synthesis (COX inhibition), most effective |
| Combined oral contraceptives | — | Alternative/add-on — suppress ovulation, reduce endometrial prostaglandin production |
| Secondary dysmenorrhea | Treat underlying cause (e.g., laparoscopic management of endometriosis) | — |

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why are NSAIDs first-line for primary dysmenorrhea?</summary>
Primary dysmenorrhea is driven by excess prostaglandin release; NSAIDs directly inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX), reducing prostaglandin synthesis and thus uterine contraction/pain at the source.
</details>

## 3. Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS)

**Definition:** Cyclical recurrence of physical and psychological symptoms during the luteal phase (1–2 weeks before menses), resolving with onset of menstruation; severe form with predominant mood symptoms = Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD).

**Etiopathogenesis:** Not fully understood — thought to involve cyclical fluctuation in oestrogen/progesterone affecting serotonin and GABA neurotransmission, rather than absolute hormone level abnormality.

**Clinical manifestations:** Physical — bloating, breast tenderness, headache, fatigue; Psychological — irritability, mood swings, anxiety, depressed mood, difficulty concentrating; symptoms must resolve by/shortly after onset of menses (key diagnostic feature distinguishing it from other mood disorders).

**Non-pharmacological management:** Regular exercise, adequate sleep, stress reduction, reduced caffeine/salt/alcohol intake, calcium and vitamin B6 supplementation (some evidence of symptom reduction), symptom diary to confirm cyclical pattern.

**Pharmacological management:**
| Symptom focus | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Mood/psychological symptoms (PMDD) | SSRIs (Fluoxetine, Sertraline) — can be used continuously or luteal-phase only |
| Physical symptoms (bloating, breast tenderness) | Diuretics (Spironolactone) for bloating; NSAIDs for pain/headache |
| Cycle suppression (severe cases) | Combined oral contraceptives |

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Key diagnostic feature distinguishing PMS/PMDD from other mood disorders</summary>
Symptoms are strictly cyclical — appearing in the luteal phase and resolving with (or shortly after) the onset of menstruation, with a symptom-free period in the follicular phase. Persistent symptoms throughout the cycle suggest an alternative diagnosis.
</details>

## Quick Revision Table

| Condition | Key diagnostic clue | 1st-line treatment |
|---|---|---|
| PCOS | Rotterdam criteria (2 of 3) | Weight loss + Metformin / COCs |
| Primary dysmenorrhea | Cyclical pain, no pathology | NSAIDs |
| PMS/PMDD | Cyclical, luteal-phase symptoms | Lifestyle measures; SSRIs for PMDD |

## Self-Test

<details>
<summary>Q1. State the Rotterdam criteria for diagnosing PCOS.</summary>
Any 2 of 3: oligo/anovulation, clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, polycystic ovaries on ultrasound.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q2. Why is weight loss considered first-line management in PCOS?</summary>
Even a modest 5–10% weight reduction improves insulin sensitivity, restores ovulation, and reduces hyperandrogenism symptoms.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q3. What is the pathophysiological basis of primary dysmenorrhea, and which drug class targets it directly?</summary>
Excess prostaglandin (PGF2α) release causing uterine contraction/ischaemia; NSAIDs (COX inhibitors) target this directly.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q4. What key timing feature distinguishes PMS from a persistent mood disorder?</summary>
PMS symptoms occur cyclically in the luteal phase and resolve with/shortly after menstruation onset, with a symptom-free follicular phase.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q5. Which drug class is used for PMDD when psychological symptoms predominate?</summary>
SSRIs (e.g., Fluoxetine, Sertraline).
</details>
