# Pharmacotherapeutics — Chapter 2(i)
## Dermatology: Psoriasis · Scabies · Eczema

> **Weightage:** 3 hrs · **3-day plan:** Day 3

---

## 1. Psoriasis

**Definition:** Chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory skin disorder characterized by hyperproliferation of keratinocytes.

**Etiopathogenesis:** T-cell mediated autoimmune process → accelerated epidermal turnover (days instead of weeks); triggers — stress, infection (streptococcal), trauma (Koebner phenomenon), certain drugs (beta-blockers, lithium), alcohol, smoking.

**Clinical manifestations:** Well-demarcated erythematous plaques with **silvery-white scales**, commonly on extensor surfaces (elbows, knees), scalp, lower back; **Auspitz sign** (pinpoint bleeding on scale removal); nail pitting; psoriatic arthritis in some patients.

**Non-pharmacological management:** Moisturizers/emollients, avoid triggers (stress, skin trauma, certain drugs), sunlight exposure (moderate), stress management.

**Pharmacological management:**
| Severity | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Mild-moderate (localized) | Topical corticosteroids, Topical Vitamin D analogues (Calcipotriol), Coal tar |
| Moderate-severe | Phototherapy (UVB), Methotrexate, Cyclosporine |
| Severe/refractory | Biologics — anti-TNF (Adalimumab), anti-IL-17/23 agents |

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: What is the Auspitz sign?</summary>
Pinpoint bleeding spots that appear when psoriatic scales are scraped off, due to thinned epidermis over dilated dermal papillary capillaries — a classic diagnostic clue.
</details>

## 2. Scabies

**Definition:** Contagious parasitic skin infestation caused by the mite ***Sarcoptes scabiei***, which burrows into the epidermis.

**Etiopathogenesis:** Direct skin-to-skin contact (or shared clothing/bedding) transmits mites; female mite burrows into stratum corneum to lay eggs → delayed **type IV hypersensitivity reaction** causes intense itching (2–6 weeks after first exposure; faster on re-infestation).

**Clinical manifestations:** Intense pruritus (**worse at night**), thread-like burrows especially in web spaces of fingers, wrists, axillae, genitals; papular/vesicular rash; secondary bacterial infection from scratching.

**Non-pharmacological management:** Treat all close contacts/household members simultaneously (even if asymptomatic), wash clothing/bedding in hot water, avoid skin-to-skin contact until treated.

**Pharmacological management:**
| Drug | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Permethrin 5% cream** | 1st line — applied whole body neck down, left 8–12 hrs, may repeat after 1 week |
| Ivermectin (oral) | Alternative, esp. for crusted/widespread scabies or outbreak settings |
| Benzyl benzoate, Sulfur ointment | Alternative topical agents |
| Antihistamines | Symptomatic relief of itching |

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why does itching sometimes worsen for 1–2 weeks even after successful treatment?</summary>
Itching is a hypersensitivity reaction to mite antigens/faeces that persists even after the mites are killed — post-scabetic pruritus, treated with antihistamines/emollients, not repeated scabicide unless live mites/new burrows are confirmed.
</details>

## 3. Eczema (Atopic Dermatitis)

**Definition:** Chronic relapsing inflammatory skin condition with impaired skin barrier function, often part of the "atopic triad" (eczema, asthma, allergic rhinitis).

**Etiopathogenesis:** Genetic predisposition (filaggrin gene mutations — impaired skin barrier), immune dysregulation (Th2-mediated), triggers — allergens, irritants (soaps, detergents), dry climate, stress, sweating.

**Clinical manifestations:** Intensely pruritic, erythematous, dry/scaly patches; **flexural distribution** in children/adults (antecubital/popliteal fossae) vs face/scalp in infants; lichenification from chronic scratching.

**Non-pharmacological management:** Regular emollient/moisturizer use (cornerstone), avoid known irritants/allergens, lukewarm (not hot) showers, mild soap-free cleansers, cotton clothing.

**Pharmacological management:**
| Severity | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Mild-moderate | Topical corticosteroids (low-moderate potency), regular emollients |
| Moderate (steroid-sparing) | Topical calcineurin inhibitors (Tacrolimus, Pimecrolimus) |
| Severe/refractory | Phototherapy, systemic immunosuppressants (Cyclosporine), biologics (Dupilumab) |
| Itching | Oral antihistamines (sedating types helpful at night) |
| Secondary infection | Topical/oral antibiotics if infected (common with *S. aureus*) |

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why are emollients considered the cornerstone of eczema management?</summary>
They restore the impaired skin barrier, reduce transepidermal water loss, and decrease the frequency/severity of flares — used continuously, not just during flares, reducing reliance on steroids.
</details>

## Quick Revision Table

| Disease | Hallmark feature | 1st-line treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Psoriasis | Silvery scales, Auspitz sign | Topical corticosteroids/Vitamin D analogues |
| Scabies | Nocturnal itching, burrows | Permethrin 5% cream |
| Eczema | Flexural, dry itchy skin | Emollients + topical corticosteroids |

## Self-Test

<details>
<summary>Q1. What is the classic distribution of psoriatic plaques?</summary>
Extensor surfaces — elbows and knees, also scalp and lower back.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q2. What is the first-line topical treatment for scabies, and how is it applied?</summary>
Permethrin 5% cream — applied to the whole body from the neck down, left on for 8–12 hours.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q3. Why should all household/close contacts be treated for scabies, even if asymptomatic?</summary>
To prevent re-infestation, since asymptomatic carriers can still transmit mites through close contact.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q4. What is the "atopic triad" associated with eczema?</summary>
Eczema (atopic dermatitis), asthma, and allergic rhinitis.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q5. What is the cornerstone, continuously-used treatment in eczema management?</summary>
Regular emollient/moisturizer use to restore the skin barrier.
</details>
