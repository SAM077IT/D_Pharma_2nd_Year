# Pharmacotherapeutics — Chapter 2(b)
## Respiratory System: Asthma · COPD

> **Weightage:** 4 hrs · **3-day plan:** Day 1

---

## 1. Asthma

**Definition:** Chronic inflammatory airway disease with reversible airflow obstruction, airway hyperresponsiveness, and bronchospasm.

**Etiopathogenesis:**
- Triggers: allergens (dust, pollen), infections, exercise, cold air, smoke, NSAIDs/beta-blockers, GERD, stress
- Type I hypersensitivity → mast cell degranulation → histamine, leukotrienes → bronchoconstriction, mucosal oedema, mucus hypersecretion
- Chronic airway inflammation → remodeling if untreated

**Clinical manifestations:** Episodic wheezing, breathlessness, chest tightness, dry cough (often nocturnal/early morning), symptoms reversible with bronchodilators.

**Non-pharmacological management:** Avoid known triggers/allergens, smoking cessation, breathing exercises, weight control, influenza/pneumococcal vaccination, written asthma action plan.

**Pharmacological management — stepwise (GINA guidelines concept):**
| Category | Examples | Role |
|---|---|---|
| SABA (reliever) | Salbutamol, Terbutaline | Quick symptom relief — used PRN at every step |
| ICS (controller) | Budesonide, Fluticosone (inhaled) | First-line controller — anti-inflammatory |
| LABA | Salmeterol, Formoterol | Add-on to ICS, never alone |
| LTRA | Montelukast | Add-on, useful in exercise/allergic asthma |
| Theophylline | — | Add-on, narrow therapeutic index |
| Systemic steroids | Prednisolone | Severe exacerbations only, short course |
| Anti-IgE / Biologics | Omalizumab | Severe allergic asthma (add-on) |

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why not use LABA alone in asthma?</summary>
LABA monotherapy is associated with increased risk of severe exacerbations and asthma-related death — must always be combined with an ICS.
</details>

## 2. Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD)

**Definition:** Progressive, largely irreversible airflow limitation associated with chronic bronchitis and/or emphysema, usually due to noxious particle/gas exposure (chiefly tobacco smoke).

**Etiopathogenesis:**
- Chronic bronchitis: chronic inflammation → mucus gland hypertrophy → productive cough
- Emphysema: destruction of alveolar walls → loss of elastic recoil → air trapping
- Main risk factor: **cigarette smoking**; also biomass fuel exposure, occupational dust, α1-antitrypsin deficiency (genetic cause in younger/non-smoker patients)

**Clinical manifestations:** Chronic productive cough ("smoker's cough"), progressive exertional dyspnoea, wheeze, barrel chest, pursed-lip breathing, in later stages cyanosis and cor pulmonale.

**Key differentiator (exam favorite — Asthma vs COPD):**
| Feature | Asthma | COPD |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Often childhood | Usually >40 yrs |
| Reversibility | Largely reversible | Largely irreversible |
| Cause | Allergic/inflammatory | Smoking (mainly) |
| Course | Episodic | Progressive, chronic |

**Non-pharmacological management:** **Smoking cessation is the single most effective intervention**, pulmonary rehabilitation, influenza/pneumococcal vaccination, nutritional support, long-term oxygen therapy in hypoxemic patients.

**Pharmacological management:**
| Class | Examples | Role |
|---|---|---|
| SABA/SAMA | Salbutamol, Ipratropium | Symptom relief |
| LABA | Salmeterol, Formoterol | Maintenance bronchodilation |
| LAMA | Tiotropium | Maintenance — often preferred first-line in COPD |
| ICS | Budesonide | Added in frequent exacerbators, combined with LABA |
| PDE-4 inhibitor | Roflumilast | Severe COPD with chronic bronchitis phenotype |
| Antibiotics | Amoxicillin, Azithromycin | During infective exacerbations |
| Systemic steroids | Prednisolone | Short course during acute exacerbations |

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Difference in bronchodilator response used to differentiate asthma vs COPD?</summary>
Post-bronchodilator spirometry: Asthma shows significant reversibility (FEV1 increase ≥12% and ≥200 mL); COPD shows little/no reversibility — obstruction persists.
</details>

## Quick Revision Table

| Disease | Hallmark | 1st-line controller |
|---|---|---|
| Asthma | Reversible obstruction, episodic | Inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) |
| COPD | Irreversible obstruction, progressive | LAMA (or LABA), smoking cessation |

## Self-Test

<details>
<summary>Q1. What is the first-line controller medication in persistent asthma?</summary>
Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) e.g., Budesonide.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q2. Why should LABA never be used as monotherapy in asthma?</summary>
Increased risk of severe exacerbations and asthma-related death when used without an ICS.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q3. What is the single most important non-pharmacological intervention in COPD?</summary>
Smoking cessation.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q4. Name a genetic cause of COPD seen in younger, non-smoking patients.</summary>
α1-antitrypsin deficiency.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q5. Differentiate asthma and COPD based on reversibility.</summary>
Asthma airflow obstruction is largely reversible with bronchodilators; COPD obstruction is largely irreversible/progressive.
</details>
