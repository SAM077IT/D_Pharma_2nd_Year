# Pharmacotherapeutics — Chapter 2(k)
## Ophthalmology: Conjunctivitis (Bacterial & Viral) · Glaucoma

> **Weightage:** 2 hrs · **3-day plan:** Day 3

---

## 1. Conjunctivitis

**Definition:** Inflammation of the conjunctiva (membrane lining the eyelids and covering the sclera).

**Key differentiator (exam favorite — Bacterial vs Viral vs Allergic):**
| Feature | Bacterial | Viral | Allergic (context) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discharge | Purulent, thick, yellow-green | Watery | Watery, stringy |
| Onset | Usually one eye first, spreads | Often starts in one eye, spreads to other | Bilateral |
| Associated symptoms | Eyelids stuck together on waking | Recent URI/cold symptoms, preauricular lymphadenopathy | Itching prominent, seasonal, atopic history |

**Etiopathogenesis:** Bacterial — *S. pneumoniae, S. aureus, H. influenzae*; Viral — most commonly **Adenovirus**, highly contagious.

**Non-pharmacological management:** Hand hygiene, avoid touching/rubbing eyes, avoid sharing towels/pillows, cold compresses for symptomatic relief, contact lens discontinuation until resolved.

**Pharmacological management:**
| Type | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Bacterial | Topical antibiotics — Chloramphenicol, Ofloxacin, Moxifloxacin eye drops |
| Viral | Supportive only (self-limiting, ~1–2 weeks) — artificial tears, cold compresses; antibiotics NOT indicated |
| Allergic | Topical antihistamines (Olopatadine), mast cell stabilizers (Sodium cromoglicate) |

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why are antibiotics not given for viral conjunctivitis?</summary>
Viral conjunctivitis is self-limiting and caused by a virus (commonly Adenovirus); antibiotics have no effect on viruses and their unnecessary use promotes antimicrobial resistance.
</details>

## 2. Glaucoma

**Definition:** Group of eye disorders characterized by progressive optic nerve damage, commonly (but not always) associated with raised intraocular pressure (IOP), leading to irreversible vision loss if untreated.

**Classification:**
| Type | Feature |
|---|---|
| Open-angle glaucoma (most common) | Gradual, chronic ↑ IOP; painless, insidious peripheral vision loss ("silent thief of sight") |
| Angle-closure glaucoma | Sudden blockage of aqueous outflow — **ophthalmic emergency**; severe eye pain, redness, blurred vision, haloes around lights, nausea/vomiting |

**Etiopathogenesis:** Impaired aqueous humour drainage (via trabecular meshwork in open-angle; physical blockage of the drainage angle in angle-closure) → raised IOP → optic nerve/retinal ganglion cell damage.

**Clinical manifestations:** Open-angle — asymptomatic until advanced (peripheral vision loss, tunnel vision late); Angle-closure — acute severe eye pain, redness, halos around lights, headache, nausea/vomiting, fixed mid-dilated pupil.

**Non-pharmacological management:** Regular eye pressure screening (especially >40 yrs, family history, diabetics), avoid activities/drugs that can raise IOP in angle-closure-prone individuals (some anticholinergics/sympathomimetics).

**Pharmacological management (open-angle glaucoma):**
| Class | Examples | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Prostaglandin analogues | **Latanoprost** | 1st line — ↑ uveoscleral outflow |
| Beta-blockers | Timolol | ↓ aqueous humour production |
| Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors | Acetazolamide (oral), Dorzolamide (topical) | ↓ aqueous humour production |
| Alpha-2 agonists | Brimonidine | ↓ production + ↑ outflow |
| Miotics | Pilocarpine | ↑ outflow via trabecular meshwork (also used to break acute angle-closure attack) |

**Acute angle-closure glaucoma — emergency management:** IV/oral Acetazolamide + topical Timolol + Pilocarpine to rapidly lower IOP, followed by definitive **laser peripheral iridotomy**.

<details>
<summary>💡 High-yield: Why is acute angle-closure glaucoma an emergency?</summary>
IOP can rise very rapidly, causing irreversible optic nerve damage and permanent vision loss within hours if not treated promptly — requires immediate pressure-lowering treatment and definitive iridotomy.
</details>

## Quick Revision Table

| Condition | Hallmark | 1st-line treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Bacterial conjunctivitis | Purulent discharge | Topical antibiotics |
| Viral conjunctivitis | Watery discharge, URI history | Supportive care only |
| Open-angle glaucoma | Painless, gradual peripheral vision loss | Latanoprost (prostaglandin analogue) |
| Angle-closure glaucoma | Sudden severe pain, halos — emergency | Acetazolamide + Timolol + Pilocarpine → iridotomy |

## Self-Test

<details>
<summary>Q1. What type of discharge distinguishes bacterial from viral conjunctivitis?</summary>
Bacterial — thick, purulent, yellow-green discharge. Viral — watery discharge.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q2. Why should antibiotic eye drops not be prescribed for viral conjunctivitis?</summary>
It's viral and self-limiting; antibiotics don't work on viruses and their misuse contributes to resistance.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q3. What is the first-line drug class for chronic open-angle glaucoma?</summary>
Prostaglandin analogues (e.g., Latanoprost).
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q4. Why is acute angle-closure glaucoma considered an ophthalmic emergency?</summary>
Rapid IOP rise can cause irreversible optic nerve damage and permanent vision loss within hours.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q5. What is the definitive treatment for angle-closure glaucoma after acute medical control of IOP?</summary>
Laser peripheral iridotomy.
</details>
